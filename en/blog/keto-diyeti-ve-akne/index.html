Does Keto Help Acne? What the 2024 VLCKD Study Found

9 min read · Health Guide · November 10, 2026

Diet-and-acne claims have circulated for years: dairy is bad, sugar is bad, glycemic load matters. Where the ketogenic diet fits into that picture has gotten less attention — at least in Turkish-language content. But a study published in 2024 was the first to tackle this question directly, with measurable data: it tracked how a very-low-calorie ketogenic protocol changed acne severity in young women with moderate acne. This article looks at the study's actual numbers, the mechanism that might explain them, and — most importantly — where this finding should not be confused with "regular" keto.

In a 2024 study published in the Journal of Translational Medicine, 31 women who followed a 45-day very-low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) saw their acne severity score (GAGS) fall by an average of 31.5% (p<0.001). But the protocol stayed under 800 calories a day and required medical supervision — it can't be directly compared to the standard keto diet.

What's the Insulin/IGF-1 Link to Acne?

Some acne researchers don't see the condition as purely a matter of bacteria and clogged pores; they argue that diet may drive sebum (oil) production and pore-cell proliferation through insulin and IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1). A 2012 review in Skin Pharmacology and Physiology discusses how a high-glycemic-load diet can raise insulin and IGF-1 levels, potentially over-activating a growth pathway called mTORC1 in sebocytes (oil-gland cells), which may in turn contribute to acne. A low-carbohydrate diet would, in theory, calm this pathway, since lower carbohydrate intake means lower insulin release. One important caveat: this is an indirect theory, built mostly on cell-culture and observational data. The review itself notes that quality randomized trials testing the diet-acne link are scarce, and the question remains contested.

That same review focuses specifically on how a Western-style diet high in glycemic load, combined with dairy consumption, triggers this pathway, pointing to evidence that milk proteins — especially whey — can directly raise IGF-1. That raises an interesting tension here: as we'll see below, a substantial part of the protein in the VLCKD study comes from whey protein, a source that would, in theory, be expected to raise IGF-1. The study's authors never address this point, which suggests the proposed mechanism may not be as clean as it sounds.

The 2024 VLCKD Study: Who Took Part, What Did They Do?

An Italian research team (Verde and colleagues), publishing in the Journal of Translational Medicine, enrolled 31 women aged 18-30 with treatment-naive moderate acne and grade I obesity (BMI 30.03-34.65 kg/m², median 33.05). Participants followed a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) for 45 days: daily energy intake stayed under 800 calories, with a macronutrient split of roughly 13% carbohydrate (under 30 grams a day), 43% protein (1.3 grams per kilogram of ideal body weight), and 44% fat. Most of the protein came from formulated supplements — whey, soy, egg, and pea protein. Notably, all 31 participants completed the full 45 days; there were no dropouts.

The Results: A 31% Drop in Acne Score

Researchers measured acne severity with a standard scale, GAGS (Global Acne Grading Scale), and quality of life with DLQI (Dermatology Life Quality Index). After 45 days, the average GAGS score fell from 24.26 to 16.48 — a 31.46% reduction (p<0.001). DLQI fell from 14.55 to 7.32, a 45.44% improvement (p<0.001). Here's what those numbers mean in practice: on the GAGS scale, a score of 19-30 counts as "moderate acne" and 1-18 as "mild acne" — so participants started in the moderate band (24.26) and ended up in the mild band by day 45 (16.48). That's not just a numeric improvement, it's a shift into a different clinical category. Over the same period, participants' average weight dropped from 88.90 kg to 81.73 kg, an 8.07% loss of body weight, with BMI falling by a similar margin.

The researchers also tracked two biomarkers: TMAO (trimethylamine N-oxide, a gut-bacteria byproduct linked to inflammation) fell by 51.97%, and dROMs, a marker of oxidative stress, dropped by 38.07% (both p<0.001). Phase angle, a measure tied to cell membrane health, rose by 8.6%. The authors attribute these findings to VLCKD's known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, suggesting the acne improvement may not come from weight loss alone — but the study has no comparison group to prove that.

What Did the Same Research Team Find in a Similar Skin Condition?

The same team that ran this study tested a similar VLCKD protocol the same year on a different skin condition: hidradenitis suppurativa — also known as "acne inversa," a chronic, painful condition involving abscesses and nodules caused by blocked hair follicles. In this pilot study, also published in the Journal of Translational Medicine in February 2024, 12 overweight or obese women followed a 28-day VLCKD (under 800 calories a day, with a similar macronutrient split). The Sartorius score, which measures disease severity, fell by an average of 24.37% (p<0.001), and quality of life improved by 44.62% (p=0.001); oxidative stress marker dROMs dropped by 21.1%, and oxidized LDL fell by 25.53%. Although this is a completely different condition from acne vulgaris, the result shows the same research group testing VLCKD across skin conditions in succession and finding results pointing the same direction — lower inflammation and oxidative stress markers. That suggests a more consistent research pattern than coincidence, though both studies remain small, uncontrolled pilot trials.

Is This "Regular" Keto?

This is the most important point to underline: the VLCKD used in the study is distinctly different from what this site describes, or from what most people mean when they say "I'm doing keto." VLCKD drops below 800 calories a day, draws most of its protein from specially formulated supplements, and is typically meant to be followed under a doctor's or dietitian's supervision, for a limited period of time. The standard keto diet has no such calorie ceiling — people eat to satiety on real, high-fat foods, and it's meant to be followed as a lifestyle over months or years, not weeks.

Why does this matter? Because the study can't separate how much of the improvement came from ketosis itself versus the rapid 8% weight loss, versus the sharp calorie restriction on its own. Obesity and rapid weight loss can improve inflammatory markers by themselves. So saying "if I do keto, my acne will clear up" claims more than this study actually shows. A more accurate read is: "a very-low-calorie, medically supervised ketogenic protocol may improve acne in a specific profile of women" — that doesn't automatically generalize to the sustainable keto diet most people follow.

What Does This Mean in Practice?

  • The evidence is real but rests on a single study. This 31-person trial with no control group is a promising first step, not a settled conclusion.
  • Don't try to replicate the protocol on your own. Dropping under 800 calories a day, especially using supplemented protein formulas, requires medical supervision; doing it unsupervised carries a risk of inadequate nutrition, electrolyte imbalance, and muscle loss.
  • Standard keto doesn't guarantee the same result. There's no separate study showing how much a more moderate carbohydrate restriction affects acne.
  • Your acne may be hormonal. Women with PCOS often experience acne, and in that case keto may work through a different mechanism — we cover that in detail in our article on PCOS and keto.
  • If you have insulin resistance, the mechanism becomes a bit clearer; see our article on insulin resistance.
  • For keto's other effects on hair and skin, see our article on hair loss on keto — the same diet can have both positive and negative effects on skin and hair.

Limitations

The study's biggest limitation is the lack of a control group: it's impossible to cleanly separate the effects of weight loss, calorie restriction itself, or spontaneous improvement over time. The sample is small (31 people), and all participants share a specific age/BMI/acne-severity profile — the findings don't generalize to men, normal-weight people, severe cystic acne, or postmenopausal women. The follow-up period is also short (45 days); there's no data on whether the acne improvement holds after the diet ends. The authors themselves describe the findings as "preliminary" and call for larger, controlled studies.

Sources: Verde et al., "Very low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD): a therapeutic nutritional tool for acne?", Journal of Translational Medicine, 22:322 (2024) · Melnik, "Nutrition and Acne: Therapeutic Potential of Ketogenic Diets", Skin Pharmacology and Physiology (2012)

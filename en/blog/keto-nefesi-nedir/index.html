What Is Keto Breath? Why It Happens and How Long It Lasts

7 min read · Health Guide · September 26, 2026

A few days into keto, a metallic, fruity, or nail-polish-remover-like smell can show up on your breath — and it worries plenty of people, usually with the question "is this dangerous?" Short answer: no, this is keto breath, a normal, temporary sign that your body has switched over to burning fat. But it's worth being clear about how it differs from a much more serious condition called diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) — covered below.

Keto breath comes from acetone your liver produces while burning fat, exhaled through the lungs — it's not permanent and noticeably fades within a couple of weeks.

Why Does Keto Breath Happen?

When carb intake drops, the liver starts breaking down fat to meet energy needs, producing ketone bodies. Two of them — beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate — get used as fuel by muscles and the brain. The third, acetone, is a byproduct the body mostly doesn't use. Because acetone is volatile, it passes into the lungs and gets exhaled — that's the characteristic breath smell; a smaller amount is also excreted in urine.

How Long Does It Last?

Keto breath is usually first noticed a few days into the diet, when ketone production is relatively high because the body is still new to burning fat. As the body adapts — using ketones more efficiently for fuel — acetone production drops. For most people the smell noticeably fades within 1-2 weeks; some sources note it can take a few weeks to disappear completely. It isn't permanent — if it persists unchanged for months, that's more likely dry mouth or another cause than deep, ongoing ketosis.

Keto Breath Is Not the Same as Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

This is the most important part of this article: the word "ketosis" sometimes gets confused with "ketoacidosis," but they're entirely different in severity.

  • Nutritional ketosis: blood beta-hydroxybutyrate typically stays around 0.5-3.0 mmol/L, with normal blood pH. This is the controlled, safe metabolic state a healthy person on keto reaches.
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): ketone levels typically exceed 3.0 mmol/L, and in severe cases can reach as high as 25 mmol/L, accompanied by a drop in blood pH (acidosis) and serious electrolyte disturbances. DKA is a life-threatening emergency and almost always involves severe or absolute insulin deficiency — most commonly type 1 diabetes, and occasionally type 2 diabetics on SGLT2 inhibitors. A healthy person isn't expected to reach this level through diet alone.

So keto breath on its own isn't an alarm sign. But if you have diabetes and the breath smell comes with high blood sugar, excessive thirst, nausea/vomiting, or rapid/deep breathing, don't wave it off as "normal keto breath" — seek medical attention to rule out DKA.

What Should You Do If You Have Diabetes?

If you have type 1 diabetes, or type 2 diabetes and use insulin or an SGLT2 inhibitor (e.g. dapagliflozin, empagliflozin), talk to your doctor before starting keto. The combination of these medications with keto can cause ketone levels to rise unexpectedly even when blood sugar is normal or only mildly elevated (euglycemic DKA) — so ketone monitoring should be part of your plan alongside glucose monitoring.

Practical Ways to Reduce the Smell

Time is the real fix, but a few things can help in the meantime:

  • Drink plenty of water — some acetone is excreted in urine, and dehydration makes the smell more noticeable
  • Chew sugar-free mint gum or use a mint mouthwash
  • Brush your teeth and tongue thoroughly and regularly — bacterial breath odor can compound the effect
  • Keep your electrolytes balanced (see our electrolyte balance article) — dehydration and electrolyte imbalance can also affect how the smell is perceived

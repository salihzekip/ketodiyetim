Keto Rash: Why It Happens and How Long It Lasts

8 min read · Health Guide · November 13, 2026

Three or four weeks into keto, someone notices itchy, reddish bumps on their back or chest and searches for an explanation. Usually they find one of two things: a one-line mention buried in a generic "side effects of keto" list, or an English-language forum thread repeating the term "keto rash" with no source attached. What rarely turns up is the fact that this reaction has an actual medical name, a published epidemiology, and two separate literature reviews from 2021 looking specifically at its link to ketogenic diets. This article walks through what those reviews actually found.

The medical name for this reaction is prurigo pigmentosa (Nagashima disease). In a 369-patient review, about a quarter of cases were triggered by a dietary change, and 40% of those had started a ketogenic diet. A separate review focused on keto-related cases found the rash appears after an average of 31 days and clears in an average of 18 days once the diet is stopped.

This Rash Has a Name: Prurigo Pigmentosa

Prurigo pigmentosa is an itchy skin reaction that mostly shows up on the trunk (chest, back) and neck. It starts as small pinkish-red bumps, sometimes fluid-filled, that develop into raised patches, usually in a fairly symmetrical pattern. Its most distinctive feature is what's left behind once it heals: a net-like (reticulated) dark pigmentation mark, which is where the "pigmentosa" in the name comes from. It was first described in Japan, which is why it's also known in the literature as Nagashima disease, but the link to diet — and to keto specifically — is a more recent pattern, one that's tracked the rise in keto's popularity.

Who Gets It? The Numbers Behind a 369-Patient Review

Mufti and colleagues published a systematic review in JAAD International in 2021 that pulled together 115 studies from 24 countries, covering 369 patients. 72.1% (266/369) were women, with an average age of 25.6 (range 13-72). A clear trigger or aggravating factor was identified in 52.3% (193/369) of patients; of those, 25.5% (94/369) were linked to a dietary change, and within that group, 40.4% (38/94) had specifically started a ketogenic diet. So the rash isn't limited to people doing keto — but keto is the single most common identifiable dietary trigger.

The most frequently used treatment was minocycline alone (20.9%, 77/369); according to the article's abstract, it led to complete resolution in 48.1% (37/77) of those patients (the results section of the same paper gives a slightly different figure, 45.5% — a minor inconsistency, but both numbers point the same way: minocycline tends to work).

Smaller studies looked at this before the 2021 review. A 2017 review by Vargas-Mora and colleagues pooled 210 cases, finding an average age of 24.4, with 84.3% of cases between 11 and 30 years old, and a female-to-male ratio of 2.6:1. The two reviews are nearly four years apart, but the picture is consistent: this is a condition that shows up more in young adults, and more in women.

Keto Isn't the Only Trigger

A dietary change is only one piece of the identifiable triggers. In Mufti and colleagues' review, friction, excessive sweating and ketonuria (ketones detected in urine) were also listed among the reported triggers. That suggests the condition isn't exclusive to the ketogenic diet — it may be linked more broadly to states where ketone levels rise in the body or the skin becomes irritated. Similar rashes have been reported in people with type 1 diabetes who develop ketoacidosis, or in people who fast for extended periods — an indirect clue supporting the ketosis hypothesis, though not proof of it.

How Strong Is the Link to Keto, and What's the Mechanism?

Worth being honest here: the exact mechanism still isn't known. The leading hypothesis is that it's tied to ketosis itself, rather than an allergic reaction to any specific food — in other words, a byproduct of the metabolic state of burning fat for fuel. Some authors have suggested a role for rapid blood sugar swings or acidity in sweat; others suspect an autoimmune component. None of this is a confirmed mechanism — it's still at the hypothesis stage.

A meaningful share of case reports, especially those from outside Asia, have increased in recent years alongside keto's growing popularity. Whether that reflects more actual cases or just more recognition (and therefore more reporting) is hard to untangle. Mufti and colleagues' review makes exactly this point: prurigo pigmentosa is frequently misdiagnosed and patients end up on ineffective treatments, largely because most clinicians see it rarely or don't recognize it at all.

Timing: When It Appears, When It Clears

Xiao and colleagues published a review in the Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology in 2021 focused specifically on keto-diet-related cases, pulling together details from 19 patients. The rash appeared anywhere from 6 days to 4 months after starting the diet, averaging 31 days. Once the diet was stopped, it cleared in an average of 18 days. Stopping or loosening the diet was part of treatment in every case reviewed; about half (10/19) also received oral doxycycline, dosed at 100-200 mg a day for 2 weeks to 2 months. After healing, 68% of cases were left with residual dark pigmentation; 42% already showed hyperpigmentation at the time they were first seen — meaning in some cases the early itchy phase goes unnoticed and the mark is what gets reported first.

What Else Could It Be, and When to See a Doctor

Prurigo pigmentosa has a fairly distinctive look, but in its early stage it can be confused with hives (urticaria), irritant contact dermatitis, confluent and reticulated papillomatosis, or pityriasis rosea. Self-diagnosing "this is definitely keto rash" isn't a safe shortcut — allergic eczema, for instance, tends to show up on the hands, elbows and knees, while prurigo pigmentosa concentrates on the trunk, which is a useful rough clue but not a substitute for an actual diagnosis. If the rash comes with fever, widespread swelling, or breathing difficulty, or if it lasts longer than a few weeks, see a dermatologist. People with a history of autoimmune disease (such as Sjögren's syndrome) and pregnant individuals are advised to be especially cautious, since both the course of the rash and the treatment options can differ for these groups.

What Should You Actually Do?

  • Don't panic, but don't ignore it either. Prurigo pigmentosa doesn't affect internal organs and isn't a serious health threat, but the itching can be genuinely uncomfortable and it tends to leave a mark, so catching it early is worth it.
  • See a dermatologist. A skin exam is far more reliable than self-diagnosis for confirming it's actually prurigo pigmentosa and ruling out the look-alikes listed above.
  • You don't have to quit keto — but you may need to make a call. In most cases, loosening or stopping the diet speeds up healing. If you'd rather stay on keto, talk to your doctor about an antibiotic option like doxycycline or minocycline.
  • Try a gradual adjustment instead of dropping carbs to zero. In some case reports, raising net carbs temporarily to a moderate range (around 50 grams a day) was enough to calm symptoms without fully exiting ketosis — this is a balance that varies from person to person.
  • Don't confuse it with other keto side effects. Fatigue and headaches in the first weeks are usually about keto flu, hair loss comes from a different mechanism entirely (covered in detail here); the skin rash doesn't share a root cause with either.
  • Track how it progresses. Noting or photographing where the rash is, how big it is, and when it started helps both at the dermatologist's office and when matching the timing against your diet — the trigger gets harder to pin down once weeks have passed.

Limitations

Both reviews cited here pool case reports and small case series from around the world; neither is a randomized trial, and neither has a control group. That creates two real limitations. First, there's no way to calculate a true population incidence from this data — how many people doing keto actually get this rash — because these are only "reported case" counts; mild or unnoticed cases most likely never make it into the literature at all. Second, a substantial share of reports come from East Asia, and it's genuinely unclear whether that reflects a geographic or genetic predisposition or simply better recognition of the condition in that region. The 19-patient sample in Xiao and colleagues' review is also small in statistical terms, so the average timing figures (31 days, 18 days) should be read as a rough tendency rather than a precise forecast.

Sources: Mufti A et al., "Clinical manifestations and treatment outcomes in prurigo pigmentosa (Nagashima disease): A systematic review of the literature", JAAD International (2021) · Xiao A et al., "Ketogenic Diet-induced Prurigo Pigmentosa (the 'Keto Rash'): A Case Report and Literature Review", Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology (2021)

Does Keto Increase Gallstone Risk?

8 min read · Health Guide · October 31, 2026

Most people starting keto notice something unexpected in the first couple of weeks: the weight comes off fast, sometimes 3-4 kilos in the first ten days. Most of that is water, but early fat loss is real too. That rapid drop feels motivating, but nutrition research has long linked rapid weight loss itself to a higher risk of gallstones. The question is whether that link holds for keto specifically, or whether it's really a generic "low-calorie diet" finding. There isn't much content anywhere that tackles this directly for keto dieters — most search results either cover general gallstone facts or general diet advice unrelated to keto. This piece walks through the actual studies, the one keto-specific case report, and practical ways to lower the risk.

In a 1989 study of 51 people, 25.5% developed new gallstones within 8 weeks of starting a low-calorie diet, while a non-dieting control group had none. A large 2014 cohort study found that people on a very-low-calorie diet had a 3.4-times higher risk of gallstones requiring hospital care than people on a more moderate low-calorie diet. Evidence specific to keto itself is limited to a single case report.

Why Does the Gallbladder Come Under Strain During Weight Loss?

The gallbladder stores bile made by the liver and squeezes it into the intestine when you eat, to help digest fat. During rapid weight loss, two things happen at once. First, the body mobilizes cholesterol from fat tissue faster than the bile acids can keep up, so bile becomes supersaturated with cholesterol, which starts to crystallize. Second, on diets that are very low in fat or very low in total calories, the gallbladder doesn't contract and empty as often, so bile sits and concentrates in the gallbladder, giving stone nuclei more time to grow. Together, these two mechanisms are why rapid weight loss is a known gallstone risk factor.

The 1989 Study: The Early Effect of a Low-Calorie Diet

The oldest study on this topic that's still widely cited is the one by Liddle and colleagues, published in Archives of Internal Medicine. 51 obese adults went on a low-calorie diet, while a separate group of 26 people were followed without dieting. After 8 weeks, ultrasound scans showed new gallstones in 25.5% (13 people) of the dieting group, plus gallbladder sludge in 3 more. None of the non-dieting control group showed any change. One person in the diet group developed biliary colic and needed gallbladder surgery. The authors concluded that this kind of weight-loss dieting predisposes people to gallstone formation.

It's worth noting this study tested a low-calorie diet in general, not a ketogenic one specifically. So the finding is tied more to calorie restriction and the pace of weight loss than to macronutrient composition. Still, that matters for keto, since the early weeks of keto also involve a similarly fast drop in weight.

The 2014 Cohort Study: Faster Loss, Higher Risk

A larger and more recent piece of evidence comes from a matched cohort study by Johansson and colleagues, published in 2014 in the International Journal of Obesity, following people enrolled in a commercial weight-loss program in Sweden. The researchers matched 3,320 people on a very-low-calorie diet (roughly 500 calories a day) against 3,320 people on a low-calorie diet (1,200-1,500 calories a day), matching by age, sex, BMI, waist circumference and gallstone history, then tracked both groups for a year through Sweden's national patient register.

The result was clear: the very-low-calorie group had 152 gallstone events requiring hospital care per 10,000 person-years, versus 44 in the low-calorie group (hazard ratio 3.4, 95% CI 1.8-6.3, p<0.001). The rate of gallbladder removal surgery followed a similar pattern (hazard ratio 3.2). When the researchers adjusted for how much weight people lost in the first three months, the difference shrank but didn't disappear (hazard ratio 2.5) — meaning part of the effect comes from the amount of weight lost, and part from the pace and type of the diet itself. The authors also noted that the absolute risk was low; this isn't a "everyone gets stones" finding, it's a relative risk increase.

Evidence Specific to Keto: A Single Case Report

Everything above tested general calorie restriction, not a ketogenic diet by name. The only direct evidence naming a ketogenic diet specifically is a 2014 case report in Pediatric Neurology. A 5-year-old with drug-resistant epilepsy developed symptomatic gallstones about a year after starting a ketogenic diet for seizure control, and had a laparoscopic gallbladder removal. He tolerated surgery well and continued the diet afterward with no further gallbladder issues. The authors noted this was the first report describing a patient continuing a ketogenic diet after gallbladder removal.

A single case report has real limits — it's one observation, not a rate or a risk estimate; it only tells you "this can happen." There's currently no study directly measuring gallstone incidence in free-living adults following a ketogenic diet. That's the biggest evidence gap on this topic.

Keto's High Fat Content Might Actually Be Protective

Here's an interesting wrinkle. A 2014 meta-analysis in Clinical Gastroenterology and Hepatology pooled 13 randomized trials (1,836 participants) looking at what reduces gallstone formation during weight loss. It found that both ursodeoxycholic acid use and higher-fat diets reduced gallstone formation compared with low-fat diets — the higher-fat diets came out with a risk ratio of 0.09, a striking reduction. The mechanism makes sense: dietary fat triggers the gallbladder to contract and empty regularly, which keeps bile from sitting still long enough to form stones.

That's somewhat reassuring for keto: its high fat content could, in theory, be protective by keeping the gallbladder emptying on a regular schedule. The real issue on keto is probably not the fat itself, but the speed of weight loss in the first weeks, and the fact that some very-low-calorie keto variants may not include enough regular fat intake per meal.

Practical Ways to Lower the Risk

  • Don't push the pace of weight loss too hard. Sustained loss beyond roughly 1-1.5 kg a week is the threshold flagged in the research as raising gallstone risk. Avoiding very-low-calorie keto variants (under about 800 calories a day) and watching total calories, not just macros, makes sense here.
  • Make sure meals contain enough fat. Fat is what triggers the gallbladder to empty regularly; skipping meals or running a very low-fat version of keto works against that.
  • Drink enough water and keep electrolytes balanced. This is also part of general keto adaptation in the early weeks (see our electrolyte balance guide).
  • If you have risk factors, talk to a doctor first. Being female, being over 40, having obesity, a personal history of gallstones, or a family history of gallbladder surgery are all recognized risk factors. If that fits you and you're planning rapid weight loss, it's worth discussing a preventive option like ursodeoxycholic acid with your doctor beforehand — this is a prescription medication, not something to start on your own.

Limitations

The biggest gap here is the lack of a study directly measuring gallstone incidence in free-living adults on a ketogenic diet, with a comparison group. The Liddle (1989) and Johansson (2014) studies test the speed and degree of calorie restriction, not ketogenic macronutrient composition, so applying their findings to keto is an indirect inference. The only direct keto-specific evidence is a single pediatric epilepsy case — not enough to estimate a rate. The Johansson study is observational, not randomized; participants chose and paid for their program themselves, which can introduce selection effects. Some trials in the Stokes meta-analysis were conducted in a bariatric-surgery context, which may not reflect typical keto dieting. In short: the link between rapid weight loss and gallstones is well established, but how large that effect is specifically for keto remains unknown.

Sources: Liddle RA, Goldstein RB, Saxton J., "Gallstone formation during weight-reduction dieting", Archives of Internal Medicine (1989) · Johansson K, et al., "Risk of symptomatic gallstones and cholecystectomy after a very-low-calorie diet or low-calorie diet in a commercial weight loss program: 1-year matched cohort study", International Journal of Obesity (2014) · Desai N, et al., "Management of symptomatic cholelithiasis while on ketogenic diet: a case report", Pediatric Neurology (2014) · Stokes CS, et al., "Ursodeoxycholic acid and diets higher in fat prevent gallbladder stones during weight loss: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials", Clinical Gastroenterology and Hepatology (2014)

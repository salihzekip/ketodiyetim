Hair Loss on Keto: Why It Happens and When It Stops

7 min read · Health Guide · September 2, 2026

Seeing more hair than usual in the brush two or three months into keto is genuinely alarming. The good news: it's usually not permanent hair loss at all — it's telogen effluvium, a temporary response where the body reads a sudden change as a stress signal and pushes a portion of hair follicles into an early resting phase. Get the nutrition right and most cases resolve on their own within 3-6 months.

Hair loss usually isn't caused by keto itself — it's caused by pairing keto with an aggressive calorie cut at the same time.

Is Hair Loss Actually Caused by Keto?

Normally about 85-90% of hair follicles sit in the growth (anagen) phase, with the rest resting (telogen). When the body registers a significant stressor — a sudden calorie drop, illness, surgery, postpartum recovery, a major diet change — it pushes a chunk of follicles that would normally still be growing into telogen early. This isn't unique to keto; it can happen with any abrupt diet shift. What makes keto a specific risk factor is that most people cut carbs and slash total calories hard in the very same week.

Why Does It Show Up 2-3 Months Later?

This is the part that confuses people. There's a 6-12 week lag between the trigger and visible shedding, because a follicle that enters telogen sits for a while before it actually sheds. So if someone says "I've been on keto for 10 weeks, that can't be related" — that's actually right in the expected window. Because of that lag, most people never connect the dots and instead blame whatever changed most recently, like a new shampoo or the weather.

Who's More Likely to Experience It?

  • Women: higher iron requirements and hormonal cycling make women more prone to this kind of stress-triggered shedding
  • Anyone who starts keto alongside an aggressive calorie deficit: switching macros is one thing, but cutting calories hard in the same week compounds the risk
  • People with low protein intake: focusing on fat and neglecting protein is a problem for hair too — a follicle is largely keratin, which is protein
  • Anyone with borderline-low ferritin already: especially women with heavy periods, whose ferritin may already be near the low end before they even start

What Actually Lowers the Risk?

Honestly, there's no magic fix, but a few concrete steps make a real difference. First: don't combine starting keto with an aggressive calorie cut in the same week — space the two out instead of stacking them. Second, track your protein — aiming for 1.2-1.6g per kilogram of bodyweight covers what both muscle and hair need. Third, don't skip iron and zinc sources: red meat, egg yolks, mussels, pumpkin seeds, and spinach are the obvious ones. Last, don't push total calories too low — keto already suppresses appetite on its own, no need to turn that into a deliberate crash diet on top of it.

Does Biotin Supplementation Help?

If you have an actual biotin deficiency, yes, it makes a difference. But in practice, the real issue in most cases isn't biotin at all — it's insufficient protein or total calories. Fixing what's on your plate is usually more decisive than reaching for a supplement bottle first.

When Should You See a Doctor?

If shedding goes past 6 months, shows up in patches instead of being spread evenly, or comes alongside fatigue, cold intolerance, or a sudden weight change — don't chalk it up to keto alone. Get bloodwork done, including ferritin and TSH; a thyroid issue or a depleted iron store can exist independently of the diet and simply become visible at the same time.

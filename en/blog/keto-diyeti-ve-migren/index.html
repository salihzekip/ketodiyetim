Can a Ketogenic Diet Reduce Migraines? What the Clinical Studies Found

8 min read · Health Guide · October 10, 2026

Migraine is more common than most people assume. A nationwide home-based study found migraine in roughly one in six adults, with the rate climbing to around one in four women and close to one in ten men. For a group this large, "can diet actually reduce migraines" isn't a throwaway question. Yet most popular content on the topic either frames carb restriction as a migraine trigger or skips the subject entirely. In the last decade, though, a handful of neurology clinics have actually tested this with real, if modest, clinical studies.

In one study of drug-resistant chronic migraine patients, monthly migraine days dropped from 30 to 7.5. In a separate 2015 study of overweight women with migraine, the ketogenic diet group showed significant improvement by the end of the first month, while the standard-diet group did not. A 2023 meta-analysis pooled this kind of evidence and found an overall effect, but the included studies are small and use different protocols.

What's Actually Happening in the Migraine Brain?

Migraine doesn't have a single cause, but one explanation that's gotten the most attention in recent years is a "hypometabolic" state in the brain. Imaging studies suggest that in migraine patients, especially between attacks, there's a disruption in energy metabolism. A 2025 review frames this picture around a cluster of interlinked factors: mitochondrial dysfunction, an altered gut microbiome, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and changes in glucose metabolism. The rationale for a ketogenic diet rests on the idea that ketone bodies offer the brain a more stable energy source than glucose, which could partly correct that hypometabolic state.

There's an important limit here: this is a mechanistic hypothesis, not a proven cause-and-effect relationship. That same review pulls together studies reporting that ketogenic diets reduce migraine days, pain intensity, and acute analgesic use, but it presents this as a potential worth testing in larger, controlled trials — not as a settled treatment recommendation.

What the Numbers Show: Two Clinical Studies

The most concrete results come from a study published in 2021 in Neurological Sciences. Researchers examined chronic migraine patients who hadn't responded to medication and who also had medication overuse headache (where the painkillers themselves contribute to the headache). Fifty patients were enrolled, 38 completed the process, and 23 were included in the final statistical analysis. The results were striking: median monthly migraine days fell from 30 to 7.5, median attack duration fell from 24 hours a day to 5.5 hours, and the median number of monthly analgesic doses fell from 30 to 6. All three changes were statistically significant.

Earlier, a 2015 study in the European Journal of Neurology randomized 96 overweight women with migraine into a ketogenic diet group (45 people) or a standard diet group (51 people) at a diet clinic. Starting from an average of 2.9 attacks a month, the ketogenic group showed a significant reduction by the end of the first month, while the standard-diet group showed no such change. The researchers reported that the improvement continued through month 6, even though it weakened somewhat during the transition period.

These two studies have different participant profiles — one is drug-resistant, severe chronic migraine patients, the other overweight women. That makes it wrong to simply add the results together, but both point the same direction: strict carbohydrate restriction reduced migraine burden in these particular groups.

The 2023 Meta-Analysis: What Ten Studies Agree On

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Nutrition in 2023 pooled 10 studies using PRISMA methodology. The approaches used varied widely: a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet, a modified Atkins diet, a classic ketogenic diet, and direct supplementation with beta-hydroxybutyrate (ketones themselves). Despite high heterogeneity, the researchers found a statistically significant overall effect of these approaches combined on migraine frequency and severity. The same review notes they couldn't establish a clear relationship between the depth of ketosis and the benefit seen, because studies measured ketosis level inconsistently.

A similar comparison appeared in a retrospective real-world study published in the Journal of Headache and Pain: 60 patients followed either a low-glycemic-index diet or a 2:1 ketogenic diet, and both groups showed improvement in migraine frequency, severity, quality-of-life scores, and body weight. That study doesn't fully answer whether ketosis specifically matters versus general carb/calorie control, but it does show both approaches can help. That's actually a useful practical message for clinicians: if a patient struggles with a strict ketogenic protocol, a more moderate low-glycemic-index approach may offer similar benefit — especially once long-term sustainability is factored in.

Where Migraine Triggers and the Keto Food List Overlap

Some migraine patients report aged cheese, processed meat (salami, cured sausage, bacon), and alcohol — especially red wine — as triggers. The proposed mechanisms differ: tyramine in cheese, nitrates/nitrites in processed meat. This relationship varies a lot from person to person; some observational studies find a link, others don't, so it isn't a universal rule. Still, there's a practical point worth making: a standard keto food list tends to lean on processed meat and hard cheese because they're carb-free and convenient. If you have migraine and have noticed a link with these foods in the past, building your keto plate around fresh meat, chicken, fish, eggs and olive oil instead makes more sense.

One more thing worth clarifying: KetoDiyetim recommends combining keto with intermittent fasting, but the ketosis level in the studies above generally reflects a continuous, daily ketogenic diet. Occasional fasting can produce mild ketosis, but it doesn't guarantee the same effect as the sustained ketosis measured in these trials. So expecting "I fast some days, so my migraines should improve too" isn't realistic.

What to Know Before You Try This

  • Most of these studies are small, single-center, and mostly from Italy. Most participants were overweight women, so it's hard to fully separate how much of the improvement came from ketosis itself versus accompanying weight loss.
  • If you take migraine medication, never adjust the dose on your own. Whether your medication needs change alongside a diet change is something to evaluate with your neurologist over time.
  • The keto transition itself carries its own headache risk. Early "keto flu" symptoms and electrolyte loss can be mistaken for an actual migraine attack; see our electrolyte balance article for more on this transition period.
  • Dehydration is a known migraine trigger. Since keto tends to increase urination early on, paying extra attention to fluids and salt in the first weeks matters — read it alongside our one-month program article for week-by-week expectations.
  • Hormonal fluctuations are a known migraine trigger in women. If your migraines track with your menstrual cycle, it's worth reading this alongside our article on intermittent fasting and women's hormones.
  • Keep a migraine diary. Tracking attack frequency, duration, and triggers while changing your diet is the only reliable way to separate a real effect from how you feel in the moment.

Limitations

None of the studies here describe a method that "cures" migraine. Sample sizes are small (ranging from 23 to 96 people), follow-up periods are short (1 to 6 months), and the ketogenic protocols used vary considerably. Participant profiles aren't uniform either — some are drug-resistant, severe chronic migraine patients, others overweight women. A large, multi-center, randomized controlled trial doesn't exist yet. Publication bias is also worth keeping in mind: small studies with a positive result tend to get published and found more often than ones with a negative result, which can make a meta-analysis's pooled effect look somewhat rosier than reality. Today's evidence is enough to say "this approach may help a subset of migraine patients, and the mechanism is plausible" — it isn't enough to say "it works for everyone" or "it replaces medication."

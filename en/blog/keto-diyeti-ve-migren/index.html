Does Keto Help Migraine? What the Clinical Studies Actually Show

9 min read · Health Guide · October 16, 2026

Migraine is a chronic neurological condition that millions of people live with regularly, and one that gets dismissed as "just a headache" far too often, even though it genuinely erodes quality of life. Patients who don't respond well enough to standard medication have increasingly started trying keto — a way of eating that's been used for decades to treat epilepsy, and whose effect on migraine has become the subject of a small but growing body of clinical research. Here we walk through four studies, what they found, and where they fall short.

In a handful of small studies on patients with treatment-resistant chronic migraine, 3 months of a ketogenic diet significantly cut monthly headache days and medication use — in one study, from a median of 30 days down to 7.5. But every one of these studies is small, most are uncontrolled, and they were mostly done in a specific group of drug-resistant patients; a large randomized trial that would generalize to everyone doesn't exist yet.

What's Actually Happening in a Migraine Brain?

The exact cause of migraine still isn't fully understood, but imaging studies in recent years show that brain energy metabolism works differently than normal during migraine attacks and even between them — some researchers call this the "hypometabolic migraine brain." A 2025 review in Brain and Behavior that screened 50 studies (Gunasekera et al.) proposes the ketogenic diet as a treatment approach that might compensate for that energy gap, since ketone bodies are an alternative fuel source the brain can use easily instead of glucose. The same authors are also explicit that the existing studies have small sample sizes and widely differing methods, which means it isn't currently possible to directly compare or pool their results. In other words, this mechanism is a reasonable hypothesis, not a proven one.

What Changed After 3 Months of Keto in Treatment-Resistant Migraine?

One of the most frequently cited studies is an Italian trial published in Neurological Sciences in 2021 (Bongiovanni et al.). It enrolled patients with treatment-resistant chronic migraine, some with a history of medication overuse; 50 people started the study, 38 completed it, and data from 23 were used in the final statistics. After three months on a ketogenic diet, median monthly headache days fell from 30 to 7.5 (p<0.0001), median attack duration dropped from 24 hours to 5.5 hours, and the number of monthly analgesic doses used fell from 30 to 6. The authors interpreted this as an improvement in quality of life, though they didn't use a separate questionnaire to measure that directly — so that particular conclusion is an indirect inference.

Those numbers look striking, but the design is single-arm and uncontrolled: there's no placebo or alternative-diet group for comparison. That makes it impossible to say for certain how much of the improvement came from the diet itself versus the natural course of migraine over time, or from participants simply paying closer attention to their health during the study.

Do Ketones Actually Make the Difference? Keto vs. Low-Carb

The study that comes closest to answering this question is a 2022 pilot from the same research group, again in Neurological Sciences (Lovati et al.). Patients with treatment-resistant migraine were split into two groups: one switched to a ketogenic diet, the other to a low-carbohydrate diet that didn't induce ketosis. In the first arm, 13 people were on keto and 8 on low-carb; a second part of the study added 26 more to keto and 5 more to low-carb. The keto group showed a significant reduction in attack frequency, pain intensity and medication use; the low-carb group showed no such benefit. On top of that, among the patients who responded well, the amount of ketones they produced correlated with how much their headaches improved.

That finding supports the idea that it may genuinely be ketosis itself driving the effect, not simply cutting carbohydrates — but the authors themselves stress that this is a small, non-randomized pilot study, and that larger, randomized trials are needed.

Does the Specific Keto Protocol Matter? A Three-Way Comparison

A 2023 study in Nutrients (Tereshko et al.) split 76 patients with chronic or high-frequency episodic migraine into three different protocols: a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet, a low-glycemic-index diet, and a classic 2:1 ketogenic diet. Among patients who followed each protocol for at least three months, attack frequency, severity, and fatigue, as well as functional-impairment scores measured by the MIDAS and HIT-6 questionnaires, all improved significantly across all three protocols (all p-values <0.001). The drop in fatigue also correlated with the improvement in MIDAS and HIT-6 scores. The study is retrospective and single-center, but it's still notable that different keto approaches pointed in a similar direction.

Intermittent Fasting and Migraine: One Thing Worth Watching

KetoDiyetim's standard approach pairs keto with intermittent fasting, but migraine patients need a separate warning here: skipping meals and prolonged fasting are among the best-established migraine triggers — that's not something these studies addressed, it's a much older, well-established observation from the headache literature itself. If you have migraine and plan to combine keto with intermittent fasting, it makes sense to extend your fasting window gradually rather than all at once, to drink plenty of water and get enough electrolytes (see our electrolyte balance article), and to track your attack frequency with a diary in the first few weeks. For more on intermittent fasting itself, see our 16:8 beginner's guide.

What Should You Actually Do?

  • Don't stop your migraine medication on your own. Patients in the studies above reduced their medication under a doctor's supervision; starting keto by itself isn't a reason to change your treatment.
  • Talk to your neurologist before starting, especially if you have a history of medication overuse — that's exactly the group where the strongest effects were seen.
  • Avoid skipping meals, and pay attention to water and electrolytes. Hunger and dehydration are already known migraine triggers and can easily get tangled up with keto flu.
  • Keep a headache diary. Recording attack frequency, duration and the medication doses you use before and after starting the diet lets you judge objectively whether you're actually seeing a benefit.
  • Give it at least 8-12 weeks. The results in these studies came after three months of the diet; expecting results within a few weeks isn't realistic.

Limitations

All four studies share one thing: small sample sizes, ranging from 76 patients down to 21. The Bongiovanni and Lovati studies have uncontrolled or non-randomized comparison groups, which leaves it unclear how much of the observed improvement came from the diet versus the spontaneous fluctuation that's common in migraine anyway. Most of the studies were also done specifically in treatment-resistant patients who hadn't responded well to standard therapy — whether the same size of benefit would show up in milder migraine that already responds to treatment is unknown. The ongoing KETOMIGRAINE trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05313503) is testing keto in patients who haven't responded to CGRP antibody therapy; its results haven't been published yet. Without large, randomized, placebo-controlled trials, saying "keto treats migraine" is premature.

Sources: Bongiovanni et al., "Effectiveness of ketogenic diet in treatment of patients with refractory chronic migraine", Neurological Sciences (2021) · Lovati et al., "Ketogenic diet in refractory migraine: possible efficacy and role of ketone bodies, a pilot experience", Neurological Sciences (2022) · Tereshko et al., "The Effect of Three Different Ketogenic Diet Protocols on Migraine and Fatigue", Nutrients (2023) · Gunasekera et al., "The Hypometabolic State of the Migraine Brain: Is a Ketogenic Diet the Answer?", Brain and Behavior (2025)

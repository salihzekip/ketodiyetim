Can the Keto Diet Prevent Migraines? What the EMIKETO Trial Found

10 min read · Health Guide · October 13, 2026

Most people who live with migraines eventually ask the same question: would changing my diet cut down on attacks? The internet is full of suggestions on this, but the ketogenic diet's effect on migraine sits in a different place than most diet trends — it's actually been tested in a real randomized controlled trial. The results are interesting, though they don't apply equally to every migraine patient, and there's a real risk worth knowing about during the transition.

In the 2023 EMIKETO trial, the group on a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet saw monthly migraine days drop by an average of 6.4 days after 8 weeks, compared to only 2.2 days in the balanced low-calorie diet group (p=0.008). The difference was statistically significant, but the trial was small and short — details below.

The EMIKETO Trial: What the Numbers Show

EMIKETO is a randomized controlled trial run at the San Raffaele hospital in Rome, Italy, published in the Journal of Translational Medicine in October 2023. Researchers split 57 patients aged 18-65, with a BMI of 27-35 kg/m² and 8-14 migraine days a month (high-frequency episodic migraine), into two groups: one followed a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) for 8 weeks — under 800 calories a day, 30-50 grams of carbs — while the other followed a balanced, Mediterranean-style diet (HBD) at around 1500-1600 calories a day.

Results were measured at three points. At week 8, monthly migraine days fell by an average of 6.4 days in the VLCKD group versus 2.2 days in the HBD group (p=0.008). At week 12 the gap was 7.2 days versus 3.13 days (p=0.007), and at week 24 it was 6.8 days versus 3.6 days (p=0.042) — meaning the benefit held up to some degree even after patients transitioned off the strict diet (weeks 12-24 were on a balanced diet for both groups).

There's a confound worth flagging: the VLCKD group lost an average of 8.2 kg at week 8 versus 4.3 kg in the HBD group (p=0.002) — nearly double the weight loss. The trial can't cleanly separate whether the migraine improvement comes from ketosis itself or from the larger weight loss. On top of that, half of the HBD group (14/28) dropped out before completion, versus 13.8% (4/29) in the VLCKD group — that lopsided dropout rate makes the results harder to interpret cleanly.

A 76-Person Pilot Study Fills In a Wider Picture

A retrospective pilot study run at a university hospital in Udine, Italy, published in Nutrients in 2023, looked at a broader group than EMIKETO: 76 patients with chronic migraine (45 people) or high-frequency episodic migraine (31 people), each following one of three ketogenic protocols based on BMI for at least 3 months — a milder 2:1 ketogenic diet for normal-weight patients (21 people), LGID for overweight patients (41 people), and VLCKD for patients with obesity (14 people).

After three months, average monthly migraine days fell from 18.2 to 8.75 (p<0.001) — nearly cut in half. Migraine intensity (on a 0-10 scale) dropped from 8.1 to 5.4, the MIDAS disability score from 76 to 37, and the HIT-6 score from 65 to 54; all changes were statistically significant. Fatigue scores also improved significantly across all three protocols. The most common side effects were mild abdominal pain (19 people) and constipation (12 people).

This study has an important limitation: there's no control group, and it's retrospective and unblinded — so we can't rule out a placebo effect, or the effect of "patients who were already motivated enough to volunteer for dietary treatment." But the fact that it points in the same direction as EMIKETO's controlled result keeps this from being a one-off finding.

The Mechanism: Why Might This Work?

The main hypothesis researchers put forward is this: the migraine brain shows a relative "hypometabolic" state even between attacks — meaning brain cells process glucose for energy somewhat less efficiently than normal. Ketones (the alternative fuel molecules the liver produces on a ketogenic diet) give brain cells a different energy source than glucose, which could theoretically offset that energy gap. On top of that, small imaging studies suggest ketones' anti-inflammatory effects and their normalizing effect on cortical excitability (the brain's electrical activity associated with triggering an attack) may also play a role. These are all reasonable, partly supported hypotheses — not a single mechanism proven beyond doubt.

Risks That Can Trigger Migraines During the Keto Transition

There's a practical reality worth not glossing over: the fluid and electrolyte loss common in the first weeks of keto — especially drops in sodium, potassium, and magnesium — is itself a known trigger for migraine and headache (see our electrolyte balance article). That means starting keto can briefly increase attacks during the transition before any longer-term benefit appears. Skipping meals or prolonged fasting is also a known migraine trigger, so starting keto alongside an aggressive intermittent fasting schedule could raise the risk for people who are migraine-prone.

What Should You Actually Do?

  • Start the transition with electrolyte support. Increasing sodium, potassium, and magnesium intake in the first 1-2 weeks reduces the headache risk that keto flu can trigger (see our article on what to watch for on keto).
  • Consider lowering carbs gradually rather than abruptly. Most patients in the EMIKETO and Udine studies followed a medically supervised, calorie-restricted protocol; there's no guarantee a standard ad libitum keto diet delivers the same magnitude of benefit.
  • Keep a migraine diary. Tracking attack frequency, intensity, and duration before and after starting keto lets you see objectively whether a real change is happening.
  • Give it time, but don't rush to judge too early. In both studies, the significant difference only became clear after at least 8 weeks; expecting results within a few weeks isn't realistic.
  • Talk to your neurologist if you take a preventive migraine medication. Share the diet change with your doctor, especially if you're on a medication that affects metabolism.

Limitations

EMIKETO is small (57 people) and single-center; the 50% dropout rate in the HBD group weakens confidence in the results, and the trial was partly funded by a meal-kit company (New Penta), with two authors disclosing consulting income from that company — a conflict of interest worth keeping in mind. The Udine pilot study is uncontrolled and retrospective, and can't cleanly separate "the diet improved migraines" from "highly motivated patients willing to try dietary treatment selected themselves." Both rely on short-to-medium-term (3-6 month) results, with no long-term safety or efficacy data. Neither study supports a flat conclusion like "keto treats migraine" — what they show is a real, measurable benefit for a subgroup of patients, still awaiting larger-scale confirmation.

Sources: Moriconi, Caprio et al., "Very-low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) in the prevention of high-frequency episodic migraine: the EMIKETO randomized controlled trial", Journal of Translational Medicine (2023) · Tereshko et al., "The Effect of Three Different Ketogenic Diet Protocols on Migraine and Fatigue in Chronic and High-Frequency Episodic Migraine: A Pilot Study", Nutrients (2023)

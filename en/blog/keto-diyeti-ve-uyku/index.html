How Does Keto Affect Sleep Quality? What the Evidence Says

9 min read · Health Guide · November 19, 2026

Two opposite stories circulate in keto forums at the same time. One group says they've never slept this well in their life, while another complains of lying awake for hours in the first weeks. Both might be telling the truth, because the research shows the same split. Some studies find a real improvement, some small case reports describe things getting worse, and the underlying mechanism still isn't settled. This piece walks through the actual clinical studies, the real numbers behind them, and why sleep can get worse in the first weeks, rather than offering a generic guess.

Keto's effect on sleep isn't one-directional: weight loss and metabolic improvement can support sleep, while electrolyte loss in the first weeks can work against it.

What Does the Science Say?

A 2024 review in European Psychiatry looked at the literature connecting ketogenic diets to sleep disorders. The Tunisian research team found 14 articles published between 2012 and 2022, and included 8 of them in their review. Their conclusion isn't a clean yes or no: they suggest the diet may affect sleep architecture through melatonin, but in their own words the effect is "still controversed," because the mechanisms differ from study to study. That sums up where the science actually stands right now — an interesting signal, not a settled answer.

How Sleep Changed in 324 Women

The clearest numbers we have come from Italy. A 2023 study in the Journal of Translational Medicine retrospectively reviewed data from 324 women with overweight or obesity. They followed a very low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) for 31 days, and sleep quality was measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). At baseline, 42.9% of the women reported sleeping well; after 31 days, that figure rose to 65.1%. When the researchers tested which variable best explained the improvement, the only significant predictor was the percentage of fat mass lost — meaning the improvement is tied mainly to weight loss, not to the diet itself.

Two limits matter here. The study has no control group, so we don't know if the same women losing weight on a different diet would have seen a similar improvement. VLCKD is also a stricter protocol than a standard keto diet, which typically runs 1,200-2,000 calories a day, so applying these results directly to "regular keto" should be done cautiously.

A More Detailed Picture in Multiple Sclerosis Patients

More detailed, controlled numbers come from a study in people with multiple sclerosis (MS). Published in Sleep Medicine in October 2024, the trial followed 65 patients with relapsing MS for six months on a modified Atkins-style ketogenic diet (under 20 grams of net carbs a day, monitored with daily urine ketone testing). 87% of participants completed both the baseline and 6-month assessments.

Several sleep measures improved significantly: the mean Epworth Sleepiness Scale score fell by 1.9 points, and the share of participants with excessive daytime sleepiness dropped from 15.4% to 7.7%. Insomnia scores fell by 1.55 points, obstructive sleep apnea scores by 0.91 points, and restless leg syndrome scores by 1.00 point. The researchers note these improvements were independent of any change in sleep duration, and were linked to reduced fatigue and depression. This study's strength is its controlled duration and standardized scales; its limit is that the results are specific to MS patients, and whether a healthy adult would see an effect of similar size is a separate question.

The Mechanism: Melatonin, Tryptophan, Ketone Bodies

The mechanism researchers propose works like this (and it's still a hypothesis at this point, not a proven pathway): restricting carbohydrates can reduce intake of tryptophan, an amino acid used to make melatonin. At the same time, ketone bodies are thought to support melatonin release indirectly by affecting adenosine signaling. These two effects could theoretically offset each other, which may explain why some people improve and others get worse. Weight loss also offers an indirect route to better sleep by easing sleep apnea and metabolic strain — some of the improvement seen in the MS study may be coming through that channel too.

Why Sleep Can Get Worse in the First Weeks

The picture looks a bit different in the first 1-2 weeks after starting keto. Cutting carbs lowers insulin, which makes the kidneys hold onto less sodium; water, potassium and magnesium get lost along with it. That loss can make nighttime waking, restlessness, and in some people, longer time to fall asleep more likely. There's no solid study that directly quantifies the sleep component of "keto flu" with numbers, but the mechanism lines up with electrolyte imbalance — we cover that in detail in our electrolyte balance article. As a separate hypothesis, some sources suggest the sudden dietary change may briefly raise cortisol (a stress hormone) in the first days, which could delay falling asleep; this hasn't been confirmed by a controlled study, it's just a plausible explanation floating around. In practice, this phase usually eases on its own once the body adapts to ketones, typically within 2-4 weeks.

How Does Your Eating Window Affect Sleep?

For people combining keto with intermittent fasting, there's a separate question: does the eating window itself affect sleep? In a 12-week study of 50 adolescents with obesity, the group eating within an 8-hour window didn't show worse sleep quality or sleep efficiency compared with a control group on a longer window; sleep efficiency even rose in the raw data, though that difference didn't hold up after statistical adjustment. The honest takeaway here is "didn't cause harm," not "clearly improved things."

Another 12-week study in adults (a secondary analysis of the See Food Study) compared 11 people on an 8-hour window to 9 eating on their usual schedule. Total sleep duration didn't change in either group (averaging around 6.3 hours, below the recommended 7-9 hours). But the more the window was narrowed, the longer people slept by the end of the trial. A clearer result showed up in late-night eating: in the group with the narrower window, eating within 2 hours of bedtime dropped from 36% of the time to 14%, while the comparison group showed no significant change. The samples are small, so these shouldn't be read as a firm rule, but the direction is consistent — narrowing the window doesn't appear to hurt sleep, and it does cut down on eating right before bed.

Practical Steps to Protect Your Sleep

The practical takeaway from all this research isn't a complicated protocol, just a few simple habits:

  • Don't skip sodium and magnesium in the early weeks: if you're getting nighttime restlessness or cramping, check your electrolyte intake first.
  • Finish your evening meal 2-3 hours before bed: in both keto and fasting studies, cutting back on late-night eating tends to go along with better outcomes.
  • Cut caffeine after early afternoon: some people on keto drink more coffee because they feel more energetic, which can delay falling asleep.
  • Treat the first 2-4 weeks as an adjustment period: most negative effects fade on their own in this window, so give it time before concluding it isn't working.
  • Keep a simple sleep log: noting bedtime, number of wake-ups and how rested you feel makes it much easier to see the real connection between your diet and your sleep.

When Should You See a Doctor?

If adjusting your electrolytes and eating habits doesn't bring a clear improvement within 2-4 weeks, if you notice signs of sleep apnea like excessive daytime sleepiness, snoring or breathing pauses, or if you have a chronic condition like thyroid disease or diabetes, don't write off a sleep problem as just "a keto side effect." As in the MS study, some chronic conditions require the diet to be followed under medical supervision; talk to your doctor before making a major change on your own.

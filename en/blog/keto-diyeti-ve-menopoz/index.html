Does Keto Stop Menopause Weight Gain? What the Evidence Actually Shows

10 min read · Health Guide · November 7, 2026

Somewhere between the late 40s and the 50s, a lot of women describe the same thing: the food is the same, the activity level is the same, but the waistline isn't. That experience is real and tied to the hormonal transition, but "menopause causes weight gain" is an incomplete explanation on its own. So does a low-carb or ketogenic diet actually make a difference during this stage? There's plenty written about nutrition at menopause in general, but almost nothing that looks at keto specifically for this life stage using controlled research. This article takes the topic as it is — where the evidence is solid, and where real gaps remain.

The menopause transition itself isn't a direct cause of weight gain, but the hormonal shift is linked to increases in total and abdominal fat (Climacteric, 2012). The longest controlled trial on low-carb eating in this group didn't test keto but a Paleo-type diet: in 70 obese postmenopausal women, a clear fat-mass advantage at 6 months lost statistical significance by 24 months, while the triglyceride advantage held (Eur J Clin Nutr, 2014). No study has directly tested keto for hot flashes.

The Mechanism Behind Menopause Weight Gain

According to a 2012 review in Climacteric from the International Menopause Society, the menopause transition on its own can't be blamed entirely for weight gain — but the hormonal shift during this period is associated with an increase in total fat and, specifically, abdominal (visceral) fat. Falling estrogen changes how the body stores fat, shifting it away from the hips and thighs and toward the abdomen. That's not just a cosmetic issue: rising visceral fat is also tied to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular risk. The same review notes that hormone replacement therapy may partly prevent this shift in body composition, though it's still not fully clear which women are most likely to get a metabolic benefit from it.

On top of that, muscle mass also declines with age and with menopause; since skeletal muscle is a major driver of whole-body insulin sensitivity, this loss is itself a risk factor independent of estrogen decline. That overlaps with the mechanisms we cover in our article on insulin resistance — which is exactly why a low-carb diet's effect on insulin resistance is, in theory, an interesting angle during menopause too.

The Strongest Evidence on Low-Carb Eating: The Mellberg Trial

No randomized trial has tested a strict ketogenic diet over the long term in postmenopausal women. The closest and longest-running evidence comes from Sweden: Mellberg and colleagues' 2-year randomized trial, published in the European Journal of Clinical Nutrition in 2014, which tested a Paleolithic-type diet (low-carb, high protein and fat, but not as strict as keto). Seventy obese postmenopausal women, mean age 60 and mean BMI 33 kg/m², were split without calorie counting or portion restriction (ad libitum) into either the Paleo-type diet or a diet based on the Nordic Nutrition Recommendations (NNR).

At 6 months, the Paleo group had lost an average of 6.5 kg of fat mass, versus 2.6 kg in the NNR group (p<0.001) — a clear difference. By 24 months, though, that gap had narrowed: 4.6 kg versus 2.9 kg, and the difference was no longer statistically significant (p=0.095). In the authors' own words, the Paleo group's anthropometric advantage (weight, fat mass, waist circumference) wasn't sustained at 24 months. Triglycerides tell a different story: the Paleo group's drop stayed significantly larger than the NNR group's at both 6 and 24 months (p<0.001 and p=0.004, respectively). The researchers also noted that adherence to the protein target in the Paleo group was poor — a practical detail suggesting that protein intake can easily fall short when cutting carbs without planning.

Because this trial tested Paleo, not keto, its results can't be mapped onto a ketogenic diet directly; but since both diets restrict carbohydrate while raising protein and fat, it's the closest evidence available. In short: there's a clear short-term (6-month) advantage, that weight and fat-mass advantage shrinks over the long term (24 months), but the lipid benefit appears to hold.

Hot Flashes: No Direct Evidence for Keto

Hot flashes and night sweats are among the most commonly reported menopause symptoms. Claims that "keto reduces hot flashes" show up often online, but no ketogenic-diet study backs that up. The 2023 Barnard et al. trial that did report fewer hot flashes tested a low-fat, plant-based diet with added soybeans — a macronutrient profile that's essentially the opposite of keto. Because the two diets are so different, generalizing that result to keto is a mistake. Right now, there's no reliable, direct evidence that a ketogenic or low-carb diet reduces hot flashes; much of the confident-sounding content online on this point cites no source at all.

Do Ketones Affect the Menstrual Cycle in Perimenopause?

A small 2024 study by Kackley and colleagues at Ohio State University, published in PLOS ONE, surfaced an interesting finding from a different angle. Nineteen premenopausal women — mean age 34, mean BMI 32.3 kg/m² — were split into three groups: ketogenic diet alone, ketogenic diet plus an exogenous ketone supplement, or a low-fat diet. After 14 days, 43% of the keto-only group and 30% of the keto-plus-ketone group self-reported a change in menstrual frequency or intensity; no participant in the low-fat group reported any change.

That's a notable finding, but it comes with real limits: the study was very small, the data were self-reported rather than from a validated questionnaire, participants were premenopausal (not yet in the menopause transition), and the trial had a financial tie to the company supplying the ketone supplements. A follow-up study by the same team, called KAPTIVATE ("Ketogenic Approach to Perimenopausal Transition Interventions, Vitality, and Therapeutic Effects"), designed specifically for perimenopausal women, hasn't reported results yet. So there's an intriguing lead here, but no settled answer for perimenopausal or postmenopausal women.

Bone Health and Electrolytes: Why They Matter More Here

Declining estrogen at menopause also speeds up bone loss, which is why any dietary change started during this period deserves closer attention to calcium, magnesium, and vitamin D intake than usual. The protein-adherence issue in the Mellberg trial backs this up: an unplanned shift to low-carb eating makes it easy to fall short on protein or other nutrients. Electrolyte balance matters for everyone in the first weeks of a ketogenic diet, but it carries extra weight for bone health in this group specifically — we cover practical amounts in our article on electrolyte balance.

Thyroid function also comes up more often at this age; hypothyroidism and Hashimoto's are more common in women after 40, and how low-carb eating interacts with thyroid hormones is a separate thing worth paying attention to. We cover that in more depth in our article on thyroid health — if you're considering keto during menopause, it's worth reading both pieces together.

What Does This Mean in Practice?

  • Menopause doesn't "guarantee" weight gain, but it raises the odds. The hormonal shift increases the tendency to store fat in the abdomen specifically, which makes it reasonable to revisit eating and activity habits during this stage.
  • Low-carb eating's short-term benefit (6 months) looks stronger than its long-term benefit (24 months) for weight, though the lipid benefit may persist.
  • Don't expect a miracle for hot flashes from keto. There's no direct evidence here, so managing expectations matters.
  • Don't skip protein and micronutrient planning. The protein-adherence gap in the Mellberg trial points to this as the most common mistake with an unplanned transition.
  • On hormone replacement therapy? Share any diet change with your doctor, since drug-nutrition interactions vary by person.
  • Insulin resistance and thyroid health come up more often at this stage; our articles on insulin resistance and thyroid health go into both in depth.

Limitations

The biggest limitation here is actually a gap: there's no well-designed randomized controlled trial testing a strict ketogenic diet over the long term in postmenopausal women. The Mellberg trial tested a Paleo-type diet, which differs from keto; the Kackley study was done in premenopausal women, was very small (19 people), relied on self-report, and had a commercial funding relationship. For hot flashes — menopause's most frequently asked-about symptom — there's no keto-specific data at all. The goal of this article is to show what the best current evidence actually says and where it stops; a confident verdict of "keto is good/bad for menopause" isn't supportable with today's data.

Sources: Davis SR, Castelo-Branco C, Chedraui P, et al., "Understanding weight gain at menopause", Climacteric (2012) · Mellberg et al., "Long-term effects of a Palaeolithic-type diet in obese postmenopausal women: a 2-year randomized trial", European Journal of Clinical Nutrition (2014) · Kackley et al., PLOS ONE (2024)

Does Keto Raise Your Kidney Stone Risk? What the Research Actually Shows

10 min read · Research Summary · October 28, 2026

In most Turkish health articles, kidney stones get a single throwaway line in a "watch out for these risks" list about keto: "it may carry a kidney stone risk," and that's it. Which study it's based on, how big the risk actually is, which stone type shows up most, or what you could do about it — none of that gets explained. The topic actually has more than two decades of numbers-backed research behind it, mostly from children treated with the classic ketogenic diet for epilepsy.

This article summarizes current research and is not medical advice. If you have a history of kidney stones, or you experience severe flank or abdominal pain or blood in your urine, see a doctor.

What the Numbers Say: The 2021 Meta-Analysis

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Acharya and colleagues, published in Diseases in May 2021, pooled 36 studies covering a total of 2,795 patients on ketogenic diets. Over a mean follow-up of 3.7 ± 2.9 years, the pooled kidney stone incidence came out to 5.9% (95% CI: 4.6-7.6%). Broken down by age, it was 5.8% in children (95% CI: 4.4-7.5%) and 7.9% in adults (95% CI: 2.8-20.1%) — that adult confidence interval is so wide mainly because so few adult-focused studies exist, which means that 7.9% figure shouldn't be leaned on too heavily.

Most of the Evidence Comes From Children: An Important Limitation

Most of the studies in the meta-analysis looked at the classic ketogenic diet used to treat drug-resistant epilepsy. That protocol differs sharply from the everyday keto diet used for weight loss: fat intake often runs as high as 90%, the diet is traditionally started in a hospital with an initial fasting phase, and fluid intake was historically restricted on purpose. The weight-loss version of keto that adults typically follow — fat around 70-75%, no fasting phase — probably doesn't carry the same level of risk, but no large adult-specific study has measured that directly either. Reading the 5.9% figure as "the risk that will happen to anyone doing keto" would be a mistake; this is an honest limitation of what the research currently covers.

How Meaningful Is the Comparison to the General Population?

Putting the 5.9% figure in context means looking at the general-population baseline, but there's a detail worth flagging there too. Annual kidney stone incidence in the general population is reported at roughly 0.3% in men and 0.25% in women — an annual rate. The 5.9% from the meta-analysis is a cumulative figure over a mean follow-up of 3.7 years. Lining those two numbers up directly and saying "keto multiplies your risk by X" is statistically off, since the comparison isn't converted to the same time unit first and ends up overstating the apparent size of the effect. That said, even converted roughly to an annual basis, the keto-group rate still looks meaningfully higher than the general population's — it's just that no precise "how many times higher" figure can honestly be given with the data available.

Which Stone Type Shows Up Most?

Among the studies in the same meta-analysis that reported stone type, uric acid stones were the most common at 48.7% (95% CI: 33.2-64.6%), followed by calcium-based stones (calcium oxalate/calcium phosphate) at 36.5% (95% CI: 10.6-73.6%), and mixed uric acid/calcium stones at 27.8% (95% CI: 12.1-51.9%). Those wide confidence intervals signal that the figures come from a small number of studies with small samples, so they're best read as a rough tendency rather than a precise breakdown. Since the general population's stones are overwhelmingly calcium oxalate, uric acid taking the lead in keto patients stands out. Uric acid's solubility in urine is directly tied to pH: as urine acidifies (pH drops to around 5.5 or lower), uric acid starts crystallizing, while in neutral or mildly alkaline urine it mostly stays dissolved. The mildly acidic urine keto typically produces creates exactly the kind of environment uric acid stones form in.

Mechanism: How Does Urine Chemistry Change?

A study by Furth and colleagues, published in Pediatric Nephrology in 2000, prospectively followed 112 children starting the ketogenic diet at Johns Hopkins; 6 of them developed stones (0.8 cases per 100 patient-months). The researchers found that the share of patients with an elevated urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio rose from about 40% before the diet to 75% at six months. In a tested subgroup, urine citrate fell from a mean of 252 mg/24h before the diet to 52 mg/24h on it — down to roughly a fifth of baseline. Citrate normally prevents stone formation by binding calcium in the urine; the diet's mild chronic acidosis causes the kidneys to reabsorb more citrate, lowering what's left in the urine. That same acidosis also lowers urine pH, which makes uric acid crystals easier to form.

Does Potassium Citrate Actually Work?

A 2007 study by Sampath, Kossoff, and colleagues in the Journal of Child Neurology followed 195 children who started the ketogenic diet between 2000 and 2005; 13 of them (6.7%) developed kidney stones. The stone rate was 3.2% among children who received potassium citrate versus 10.0% among those who didn't (P=.049). Two years later, a follow-up study by McNally and colleagues in Pediatrics covered 313 children from 2000 to 2008. Starting in 2006, the clinic began giving potassium citrate preventively to everyone starting the diet, not just children who turned up with elevated urine calcium. The result was clear: of the 198 children given preventive potassium citrate from the start, only 4 (2.0%) developed stones, versus 11 of 105 (10.5%) among those who didn't get it (P=.003). Neither study was randomized — both rely on comparing different time periods — but seeing a similar signal across two separate cohorts from the same center strengthens the finding. No study shows the same holds at the same rate for adult weight-loss keto; some adult-focused reviews suggest the diet's typically shorter duration in adults may reduce the need for routine citrate supplementation, but that's a plausible idea, not a tested one.

What About Adult Weight-Loss Keto?

No clinical study answers this with a hard number yet, but a few medical reviews on the topic flag something worth noting: adults typically stay on keto for as long as it takes to reach a weight goal — anywhere from a few months to one or two years — while children treated for drug-resistant epilepsy often stay on it for years, sometimes over a decade. That gap in duration offers a reasonable explanation for why cumulative risk in adults might land below the pooled average, but that's a hypothesis, not something directly tested yet. The same reviews note that, given this uncertainty, routine potassium citrate for every adult doesn't currently look necessary, but it's worth evaluating individually for anyone with a stone history or planning to stay on strict keto long-term.

Who Should Be More Careful?

A personal history of kidney stones, a family history of stones, gout or chronically elevated uric acid, a lifestyle with low daily fluid intake, and use of certain diuretics are all factors that shift this risk picture. We covered how uric acid can temporarily rise in the first few weeks of keto, and why tracking it matters, in our blood test article; that tracking matters even more for anyone with a stone history. Staying strictly on keto for six months or longer is also a factor that can raise cumulative risk, since in most existing studies stones developed over years, not months.

What Can You Actually Do About It?

  • Don't skip water — spreading roughly 2.5-3 liters of fluid through the day is the simplest way to dilute urine and reduce crystallization; see the full drink list in our what-to-drink article
  • Get your electrolyte balance right — low fluid and electrolyte intake can further disturb urine chemistry, a topic we cover in depth in our electrolyte balance article
  • Don't overdo animal protein — keeping the protein share of your macros moderate can help limit both uric acid production and urine acidity
  • Get tested before starting if you have a stone history — using your urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio and blood uric acid as a baseline, a supplement like potassium citrate can be considered with a doctor's guidance if needed
  • Don't skip periodic checks on long-term strict keto — an occasional urine test past the six-month mark is the most practical way to catch a problem before it causes symptoms

When Should You See a Doctor?

Severe pain that comes in waves in your flank or back, blood in your urine, fever accompanying pain, or a burning sensation while urinating aren't symptoms "keto flu" explains — they can signal a kidney stone and call for prompt medical attention. Even without a prior stone history, any one of these symptoms should prompt a medical evaluation before you start questioning the diet.

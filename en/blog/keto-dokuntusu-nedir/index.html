What Is "Keto Rash" (Prurigo Pigmentosa)? Causes and Treatment

8 min read · Health Guide · November 22, 2026

Some people on keto notice something unexpected three or four weeks in: small, reddish-purple bumps on the neck, chest, or back, an intense itch, and marks that gradually merge into a net-like pattern. When they see a dermatologist, the first guess is often eczema or a fungal infection, because the condition is rare and barely covered anywhere, including in Turkish. But dermatology actually has a name for it: prurigo pigmentosa, commonly called "keto rash." This piece looks at the real numbers behind it, from the largest systematic review available and a keto-specific case review.

In a 2021 systematic review in JAAD International covering 369 patients from 24 countries, among those who reported a dietary change linked to the rash, only 40.4% — about one in ten of all patients — had specifically started a ketogenic diet. Prurigo pigmentosa isn't a keto-specific reaction; it's a broader skin response that carbohydrate restriction and ketosis can trigger.

What Is Prurigo Pigmentosa, and What Does It Look Like?

Prurigo pigmentosa was first described in Japan, which is why it's also known as Nagashima disease. In its early stage, small, itchy, reddish-purple bumps appear symmetrically on the neck, chest and upper back (especially between the shoulder blades). Over time these merge into a net-like (reticulated) pattern, and in some cases small fluid-filled blisters form and crust over. As it heals, the rash is usually replaced by dark brown marks (hyperpigmentation) that can linger for a while. It mostly shows up on the trunk and neck, but a minority of cases involve less typical areas like the face, scalp, or above the pubic region.

What Do the Numbers Say? The 369-Patient Systematic Review

The most comprehensive data on this comes from a systematic review by Mufti and colleagues, published in 2021 in JAAD International. The researchers pooled data on 369 prurigo pigmentosa patients from 115 separate publications across 24 countries (69.6% of these were single case reports, the rest smaller case series). 72.1% of patients were women, the mean age was 25.6 (ranging from 13 to 72), and among the 275 patients with reported race, the large majority were of Asian descent.

A triggering or aggravating factor was noted in 52.3% (193/369) of patients. The largest slice of that, 25.5% (94 patients), was a dietary change; of those 94, 40.4% (38 people) had specifically started a ketogenic diet — about one in ten of all 369 patients. Other reported triggers included friction (8.4%), excessive sweating (7.6%), ketonuria, or ketones in the urine (5.1%), and hormonal changes (2.4%). This spread shows prurigo pigmentosa isn't "keto-specific" — any process that produces ketosis, including intermittent fasting, the period after bariatric surgery, diabetic ketoacidosis, or certain eating disorders, can trigger the same reaction.

A Keto-Specific Review: 19 Cases

The source that looks most closely at the keto connection is a 2021 case report and literature review by Xiao and colleagues, published in the Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology. Beyond their own patient, the authors compiled 19 cases published between 2015 and 2021 that were specifically linked to a ketogenic diet. Ages ranged from 16 to 43, averaging 24; 79% of cases were women. The time between starting the diet and the rash appearing ranged from 6 days to 4 months, averaging 31 days. Five of the 19 cases showed the rash in atypical locations like the face, scalp, or above the pubic area, and 42% were left with lasting hyperpigmentation.

The authors' own patient was a 21-year-old man who'd lost about 20 pounds over two months on a ketogenic diet and presented with a rash that had been present for three weeks (he'd stopped the diet on his own three days before the visit). He was started on 100 mg of doxycycline twice a day, and the itching resolved within two weeks. More than half of the 19 cases in the literature also received oral doxycycline, with the active rash clearing in an average of 18 days with treatment.

Why Does It Happen? The Mechanism Still Isn't Fully Clear

The exact cause of prurigo pigmentosa is unknown, but the leading theory is that rising ketone bodies (acetone, beta-hydroxybutyrate) during ketosis, as they're excreted through sweat, irritate the skin around sweat ducts and trigger a neutrophilic inflammatory response. That fits with the fact that friction and excessive sweating were also reported as separate aggravating factors in the review above — heat, moisture, and mechanical irritation seem to be cofactors alongside ketosis itself. This isn't an allergic reaction, and there's no evidence it's tied to a specific food or to keto's particular fat-to-protein ratio.

Treatment Options and Success Rates

In the 369-patient review, the most commonly used and best-documented treatments are tetracycline-class antibiotics. Among 56 patients treated with doxycycline alone, 51.8% achieved complete resolution and 44.6% partial resolution (with a 10.7% recurrence rate after stopping treatment); the average time to resolution was 23.4 days. Among 77 patients treated with minocycline alone, complete resolution was 45.5%, partial resolution 9.1%, and the average time was somewhat longer at 34.5 days. A smaller group treated with dapsone resolved faster on average, in 14.4 days, but dapsone isn't usually a first-line choice because of its side-effect profile (notably hemolysis risk), which requires regular blood monitoring.

In a small group of 12 patients treated with diet change alone, without antibiotics, the rash resolved completely in 41.7% and partially in 58.3%, with an average time of 30 days — somewhat slower than the antibiotic regimens, but still an effective option overall.

Does Raising Carb Intake Alone Fix It?

Among the 19 cases Xiao and colleagues compiled, one 18-year-old male patient raised his daily carbohydrate intake to 90 grams, and the rash resolved within 18 days, leaving a hyperpigmentation mark behind. It would be a mistake to turn this single case into a general rule like "90 grams is the threshold" — it's the only documented example of this specific approach in the literature. But the 12-patient group above does show that a dietary change — raising carbs or dropping the diet altogether — can work on its own, without antibiotics, just somewhat more slowly.

Who Should Be More Careful?

  • Young women — both reviews show a clear skew toward women (72.1% in the systematic review, 79% in the keto-specific review).
  • Anyone entering ketosis fast and deep — ketonuria was one of the reported triggers; aggressive carb cutting and rapid weight loss may raise the risk.
  • People who sweat heavily or wear tight, friction-prone clothing — friction and sweating were separately reported as aggravating factors in the review.
  • Anyone who's had a similar rash before — case reports describe recurring episodes, especially when the person re-enters ketosis.
  • Anyone dismissing the rash as eczema or a fungal infection and self-treating with a standard cream — a wrong diagnosis can delay the right treatment, and sometimes a simple diet tweak.

When to See a Doctor

If you notice an itchy, reddish-purple rash on your neck, chest, or back in the weeks after starting keto or intermittent fasting, especially one that develops a net-like pattern and doesn't respond to a regular moisturizer or steroid cream, it's worth seeing a dermatologist. Diagnosis is usually made on the clinical picture, though a small biopsy is sometimes used to rule out other conditions. Getting the right diagnosis, rather than starting a long course of antibiotics on your own, both avoids unnecessary medication and makes sure another skin issue isn't being missed.

What Does This Mean in Practice?

Prurigo pigmentosa isn't dangerous; the real downsides are the itch and the dark marks it can leave behind. Based on the data above, there are two reasonable paths: stay on the diet and treat it with doxycycline or minocycline prescribed by a dermatologist (which achieves complete resolution in over half of patients), or ease up on carb restriction somewhat (slower, but still effective). Which path makes sense depends on how committed someone is to their keto goals and how severe the rash is — both are supported options in the literature, and neither rules out the other. As with other temporary side effects like hair shedding or keto breath, the same basic rule applies here: don't ignore the symptom, but there's no need to panic and quit the diet either.

Limitations

Most of the evidence on this topic comes from, by the 369-patient review's own account, a fairly low average level of evidence — case reports and small case series, with no randomized trials. Treatment success rates come from groups of different sizes (56 to 77 patients), meaning these percentages could shift as larger samples accumulate. We have exactly one documented case of raising carbs alone resolving the rash, so there's no established threshold for "how many grams is enough." As with any review built on case reports, publication bias is a real possibility — more unusual or severe cases tend to get published — so the true frequency of this reaction may differ from what's reported. Finally, the literature reviewed is overwhelmingly in English; cases reported in Turkish or other languages may exist without having made it into this body of research.

Sources: Mufti A et al., "Clinical manifestations and treatment outcomes in prurigo pigmentosa (Nagashima disease): A systematic review of the literature", JAAD International (2021) · Xiao A et al., "Ketogenic Diet-induced Prurigo Pigmentosa (the 'Keto Rash'): A Case Report and Literature Review", The Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology (2021)

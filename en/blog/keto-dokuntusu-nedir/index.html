Keto Rash (Prurigo Pigmentosa): Why It Happens and How It Clears Up

8 min read · Health Guide · November 25, 2026

A few weeks into keto, some people suddenly notice itchy, reddish-pink bumps across the neck, chest or back — and it's genuinely unsettling. The condition has a name in the dermatology literature: prurigo pigmentosa, better known online as "keto rash."

This article isn't medical advice. If you have a rash that isn't improving with home care, see a dermatologist — keto rash itself isn't dangerous, but it can look similar to eczema, an allergic reaction, or a skin infection.

What Exactly Is Keto Rash?

Prurigo pigmentosa is sometimes called Nagashima disease, after the Japanese dermatologist who first described it in 1971. It's a rare inflammatory skin condition that shows up as a symmetrical, itchy rash that develops a distinctive net-like (reticular) pattern over time. According to Cleveland Clinic, it isn't harmful to your health, but it can be uncomfortable and tends to recur.

How Do the Symptoms Progress?

The rash doesn't appear in its final form all at once — it moves through stages. Early on, small, itchy, red-to-purple bumps appear and can grow into larger raised patches. In the next stage, those bumps can fill with fluid like small blisters, crust over, and form the characteristic net-like pattern. In the final stage, the itching and redness fade, but dark marks (hyperpigmentation) can linger for months. The most common sites are the neck, chest and back; the face and limbs are rarely affected.

Why Does It Happen? The Ketosis Connection

The exact mechanism is still unknown, but the link to ketosis is clear: the condition can be triggered by any state where the body is running on fat for fuel — ketogenic diets, uncontrolled diabetes, prolonged fasting, the period after bariatric surgery, and anorexia nervosa all included. One hypothesis is that changes to the gut microbiome caused by carb restriction, and the low-grade inflammation that follows, play a role — but that's still a working theory, not a proven mechanism. Other triggers unrelated to ketosis have also been reported: H. pylori infection, hormonal shifts such as pregnancy, allergic reactions to metals or chemicals, and even cases linked to autoimmune conditions like Sjögren's syndrome and Still's disease.

Who Gets It More Often?

Most published cases come out of Japan and involve young women; one case report notes an average age of onset between 23 and 27. It also turns up in men, children, and people outside East Asian populations, though those cases are rarer. Cleveland Clinic describes it as rare overall, but notes reports are increasing as keto diets grow more popular.

How Do You Know It's Actually Coming From Keto?

One published case gives a concrete timeline: a healthy 26-year-old woman on a strict ketogenic diet developed the rash just three weeks after starting, and it cleared within a month of reintroducing carbs, with no medication at all. Cleveland Clinic frames the onset more loosely, noting it can appear "days or months" after starting keto, and that it doesn't happen to everyone on the diet. One useful clue for telling it apart from eczema is location: allergic eczema typically shows up in flexural areas like the inner elbows and backs of the knees, while keto rash concentrates on the trunk, neck and back.

Does Adding Carbs Back Actually Work?

Most sources agree on this much: when ketosis is the trigger, increasing daily carb intake generally resolves it. Cleveland Clinic suggests a target of at least 50 grams of carbs a day. In the case report mentioned above, the patient cleared up within a month just by reintroducing carbs, without any medication. Cases not driven by ketosis tend to resolve on their own anyway.

If adding carbs isn't an option, or the rash hasn't improved within a few weeks, doctors may suggest antibiotics such as minocycline or doxycycline, or dapsone, for their anti-inflammatory effect even when there's no infection involved. These are prescription treatments — not something to start on your own.

Supportive Care: Nutrition and Skin Routine

Some sources point to correcting deficiencies in vitamins A, B12 and C, trying probiotics or prebiotics, and considering vitamin D or fish oil for their anti-inflammatory potential — but these are supportive suggestions, not a proven fix on their own. On the skin side, a few simple habits help: lukewarm water instead of hot (heat tends to worsen itching), gentle cleansers, regular moisturizer, and sun or wind protection. Resisting the urge to scratch matters too, since broken bumps can get infected.

If You Want to Stay on Keto Instead of Quitting

Getting a rash doesn't mean you have to abandon keto entirely. Lowering carbs gradually instead of cutting them abruptly, and starting a daily multivitamin alongside the diet switch, are practical ways to lower the risk from the start. If the rash keeps coming back, shifting to a somewhat milder, moderate low-carb version instead of strict keto is also an option — worth weighing alongside the other things to watch for when starting keto. Worth noting: keto rash isn't the same thing as keto flu — they can show up around the same period, but they run on entirely different mechanisms, and it's not directly tied to other adaptation symptoms like keto breath.

When Should You See a Doctor?

See a dermatologist if the rash hasn't improved after two to three weeks of home care, keeps spreading, comes with fever or other symptoms, or you're simply unsure what you're looking at. Diagnosis is usually based on history, a physical exam, and questions about your eating pattern; a urine ketone test, bloodwork, a skin biopsy, or a patch test for allergies may follow if needed. It's also worth checking in with a specialist before starting keto if you have a history of sensitive skin — the same goes for other visible side effects of the diet, like hair loss.

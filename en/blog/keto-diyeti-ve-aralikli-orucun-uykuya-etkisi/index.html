How Do Keto and Intermittent Fasting Affect Sleep? What Studies Show

10 min read · Health Guide · November 28, 2026

Ask people who've started keto or intermittent fasting about sleep, and you'll hear two completely different stories. One group says "my eyes stayed wide open at 3am during the first weeks." The other says "for the first time in my life I'm waking up actually rested." Neither is lying — the problem is that the science isn't as tidy as either story suggests. This article looks at what we actually have in the research on keto and intermittent fasting's effect on sleep: some of it measured in real human trials, some presented but not yet peer-reviewed, and some still at the hypothesis stage.

In a VLCKD trial of 324 women, every sleep-quality subscore improved significantly after 31 days; according to Sleep Foundation's coverage, the share reporting good sleep rose from 42.9% to 65.1%. But that trial had no control group. On the fasting side, a 2021 review found no significant change on standard sleep scales.

Why Can Sleep Get Worse in the First Weeks of Keto?

When carbohydrate intake drops sharply, the body adjusts on several fronts at once. Insulin levels fall, the kidneys retain less sodium, and that leads to water and electrolyte loss — we cover this process in detail in our article on electrolyte balance. Based on observations compiled by the Sleep Foundation, trouble falling asleep, fragmented sleep, and restlessness are commonly reported during this period. Sources like Livestrong note this may also be linked to "keto flu," increased hunger, and, in some people, a conscious or unconscious rise in caffeine intake to compensate. The good news is that this usually isn't permanent: some sources say it eases within a few days, others say it can take up to four weeks.

Do Ketones Boost Slow-Wave Sleep? Still Just a Hypothesis

A claim you'll often hear in the keto community is that ketones increase deep, slow-wave sleep. This traces back to a theoretical review published in 2021, whose authors proposed that some of the ketogenic diet's therapeutic effects might work through enhancing slow-wave sleep and resynchronizing the circadian rhythm. It's worth stressing: this is a hypothesis paper, not a clinical finding directly tested in humans. An older study in children with epilepsy offers an interesting data point from a different angle: the ketogenic diet reduced total sleep time while preserving slow-wave sleep and increasing REM sleep. That points to a "less sleep but better quality" pattern — though how much an effect seen in children with epilepsy translates to healthy adults is a separate question.

324 Women, VLCKD, and Sleep Quality: The 2023 Italian Trial

Published in the Journal of Translational Medicine in 2023, Barrea and colleagues' study is the largest human dataset we have on this topic. 324 women with overweight or obesity were assessed with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) before starting a very low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) and again after 31 days. The result: every PSQI subscore improved significantly (p<0.001). According to Sleep Foundation's coverage of the study, the share of participants reporting good sleep quality rose from 42.9% at baseline to 65.1% after 31 days.

The researchers also looked at what explained the change: in their regression analysis, the reduction in fat mass was the only independent predictor of the sleep improvement, and ROC analysis showed that a drop in fat mass greater than 8.4% predicted it. So the study's own interpretation isn't "the diet directly improved sleep," but rather "the fat loss the diet produced may have improved sleep." There's an important limitation here: the study had no control group, was single-center and open-label, lasted only 31 days, and included only women — so it's too early to claim causation.

The Picture Is Murkier on the Fasting Side

When it comes to intermittent fasting, the evidence sits in a different place than it does for keto. A 2021 review in Nutrients by McStay and colleagues summarized human trials of time-restricted eating (TRE) and alternate-day fasting (ADF) in overweight or obese adults. The finding: PSQI-measured sleep quality and duration showed no significant change across these trials. Effects on sleep latency and sleep efficiency were inconsistent between studies — worsening in one, unaffected in others. The authors add an important methodological caveat: none of the trials they reviewed were adequately powered to detect a statistically significant difference in sleep outcomes. So concluding "intermittent fasting doesn't affect sleep" is just as premature as claiming it does — the existing trials weren't designed to answer that question.

The 37,545-Person ZOE Dataset: Big, But Read With Care

At the 2023 Federation of European Nutrition Societies (FENS) conference in Belgrade, King's College London researchers presented data from 37,545 users of the ZOE health app. After eating normally for a baseline week, participants narrowed their daily eating window to 10 hours for two weeks (roughly 14 hours of fasting a day). According to King's College's press release, participants self-reported improvements in mood, energy, hunger, and sleep during this period, and those who ate within the same window every day reported more benefit than those who varied it from day to day.

This is an exciting dataset, but it needs a careful read. First, it's a conference abstract; FENS abstracts generally don't go through the peer-review process that journal articles do. Second, the sample is self-selected app users; the most engaged subgroup was mostly female with an average age around 60, which limits how far the findings generalize. Still, the idea that a consistent eating window may be more helpful than a variable one lines up with the logic we lay out in our 16:8 guide to intermittent fasting.

What's Been Found in Specific Patient Groups?

A handful of small clinical trials have looked at how a ketogenic diet changes sleep-related measures in specific patient groups. In a 6-month ketogenic diet trial in people with relapsing multiple sclerosis, 45 participants enrolled and 39 completed the study; self-reported sleep quality and sleep-disorder symptoms were assessed, and the researchers reported a benefit to sleep quality. In a retrospective 6-month Italian pilot study in people with chronic migraine, a 2:1 ketogenic diet was compared with a low-glycemic-index diet, and the ketogenic group showed improved sleep quality and daytime sleepiness. What these studies share is a small sample, a short duration, and, in most cases, no control group — so the findings are real but not enough to claim "this happens for everyone." A 2024 review summing up this picture describes the ketogenic diet's effect on sleep disorders as "controversial," since the underlying mechanisms vary by condition.

What Should You Actually Do?

  • Don't panic if sleep gets worse in the first 1-2 weeks. This is usually part of a temporary adjustment period, often tied to electrolyte and fluid balance.
  • Pay attention to salt, potassium, and magnesium intake. Electrolyte loss is a common trigger for both "keto flu" and sleep disruption; our article on electrolyte balance covers practical amounts.
  • Try to keep your last meal time consistent. The strongest signal in the ZOE dataset was that consistency mattered more than variability; we go into this further in our article on fasting meal timing.
  • Cut back on caffeine in the afternoon and evening. Rising coffee or tea intake to manage hunger during the day, especially later in the day, can make it harder to fall asleep at night.
  • Don't wait out severe or long-lasting insomnia. Anything beyond three months falls outside the "diet transition" pattern and needs its own medical evaluation.
  • If you already have sleep apnea or chronic insomnia, talk to your doctor before changing your diet; none of the studies above are large enough to represent those patient groups.

Limitations

Most of the evidence on this topic comes from small, short-term trials that mostly lack a control group. The largest VLCKD trial (324 people) has no control arm and only studied women; the largest dataset, from ZOE and King's College London, is a conference abstract that hasn't been peer-reviewed yet and comes from a self-selected sample. The intermittent fasting review explicitly states that the trials it covered weren't adequately powered for sleep outcomes. In short: the observational evidence that keto can disrupt sleep in the early weeks is fairly solid, but the evidence for whether it improves sleep long-term is still preliminary. Randomized, adequately powered, longer-term trials are needed before drawing firm conclusions.

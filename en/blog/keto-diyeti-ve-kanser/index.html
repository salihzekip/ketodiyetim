Does Keto Treat Cancer? What the Research Actually Shows

9 min read · Health Guide · November 16, 2026

Some corners of the internet talk about the ketogenic diet as if it "starves" cancer cells into submission. Other corners dismiss the idea outright. The real picture sits somewhere else: over the past decade a handful of actual clinical studies have tested the question, and the results are both more modest than the hype suggests and wildly different depending on the type of cancer involved. One mouse study even found the same diet boosting a drug's effect against one cancer while speeding up another. In this piece we walk through three studies rarely covered in this much detail in Turkish-language content: a randomized trial in recurrent glioblastoma, a mechanistic mouse study on insulin, and a feasibility study from the UK.

In the ERGO2 trial, adding a ketogenic diet plus intermittent fasting to re-irradiation for recurrent glioblastoma produced no significant difference in 6-month progression-free survival compared to a standard diet (20% versus 16%). A separate mouse study found that a ketogenic diet given alone actually accelerated disease in some leukemia models. The evidence is early-stage and highly dependent on cancer type; this diet does not replace standard treatment under any circumstance.

Where the Idea Comes From

Most cancer cells rely more heavily on glucose for energy than normal cells do, an observation that has been part of oncology research for over a century. The theory goes that a ketogenic diet, by keeping blood sugar and circulating insulin low, could turn that glucose dependence into a weak point. That's the logic behind the simplified claim that keto "starves" cancer. Real biology is messier: the body works to keep blood sugar stable through glucose released from the liver and several hormonal pathways, and some cancer cells can switch to alternative fuel sources anyway. The mechanism is a reasonable hypothesis, not one proven to hold the same way across every cancer type.

ERGO2: A Randomized Controlled Trial in Recurrent Glioblastoma

The strongest human data on this question comes from the ERGO2 trial, run in Germany. Published in 2020 in the International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics, it enrolled 50 patients with previously treated, recurrent malignant glioma, including glioblastoma. Patients were randomized 1:1: one group continued an unrestricted, calorically free diet alongside re-irradiation, while the other switched to a calorie-restricted ketogenic diet combined with intermittent fasting, cycling 3 days of keto, 3 days of fasting, then 3 more days of keto.

The primary endpoint, 6-month progression-free survival (PFS6), came out at 20% in the keto-fasting group versus 16% in the control group, a difference that was not statistically significant. No meaningful difference showed up in overall progression-free survival, local PFS6, or overall survival either. In other words, the trial's central question — does this diet improve the effectiveness of re-irradiation — got answered with a no, at least at this scale.

The diet itself looked safe: of the 20 patients who completed the protocol, 17 had reached ketosis by day 6, glucose levels dropped significantly, and no serious diet-attributable adverse events were reported. One detail complicates the comparison, though: the control group ended up eating less than planned (about 21 kcal/kg/day on average instead of the targeted 30), meaning the two diets were less different in practice than the trial design intended. A secondary, exploratory analysis flagged lower blood glucose as potentially associated with better prognosis, but that's a hypothesis-generating observation that would need its own dedicated study to confirm.

The Mouse Study Warning: Same Diet, Opposite Outcome by Cancer Type

A 2018 paper in Nature, from researchers at Weill Cornell Medicine and Columbia University (the Cantley lab), approaches the question from a different angle. The team noticed that cancer drugs targeting the PI3K signaling pathway, such as buparlisib, run into a built-in problem: they raise blood glucose and insulin, and that rise in insulin can reactivate the very pathway the drug is trying to shut down, undercutting the drug's own effect.

In mice placed on a ketogenic diet, that drug-induced insulin spike was largely blunted, and the drug's tumor-shrinking effect improved significantly as a result. The researchers tested the same idea with metformin and SGLT2-inhibitor drugs; the ketogenic diet and the SGLT2 inhibitors suppressed the insulin spike about equally well, while metformin was less effective at it.

The study's real warning sign is this: when the same researchers tested the ketogenic diet alone, without any drug, across several different tumor models, it had little effect on most of them. More strikingly, in some models of acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the ketogenic diet by itself actually accelerated disease progression. The authors concluded that in patients taking PI3K-inhibitor drugs, diet needs to be carefully managed under medical supervision. This is mouse data and hasn't been confirmed in people, but it's a concrete demonstration that the same diet can produce opposite effects depending on the cancer type and whatever treatment it's paired with.

What KEATING and Other Pilot Studies Show

In the UK, the Cancer Research UK-funded KEATING study compared two types of ketogenic diet in glioblastoma patients as a feasibility study — meaning the question wasn't efficacy yet, but whether the study could even be run and whether patients could stick to the diet. The research team reported that the trial was feasible and no major safety signal emerged, but recommended that future trials run the diet phase for 6 weeks rather than 3 months, a sign of how hard strict long-term adherence turned out to be in practice.

A separate, smaller prospective study across several cancer types ran into a similar adherence problem: of 18 patients enrolled, only 6 stuck with the diet for longer than six months. That points to a real-world difficulty — appetite loss, taste changes, and treatment side effects can make sustaining a ketogenic diet considerably harder for cancer patients than for healthy individuals. A 2025 scoping review surveying several dozen publications, mostly on brain, breast, and ovarian cancer, suggested the diet may play a supportive role in some patients' treatment course, while acknowledging that most of the underlying studies are small, observational, or uncontrolled.

What This Means in Practice

  • Keto doesn't replace standard cancer treatment. The strongest randomized evidence (ERGO2) showed no clear survival benefit; there's no scientific support for dropping chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted drug treatment in favor of this diet.
  • Cancer type and concurrent treatment matter enormously. Mouse data shows the same diet can help alongside a specific drug (like a PI3K inhibitor) while being risky alone in certain leukemias — which makes broad generalizations unreliable.
  • Sticking to the diet is its own challenge for cancer patients. Appetite loss, taste changes, and treatment side effects make strict adherence harder than it is for a healthy person, as the low completion rates in KEATING and similar pilots show.
  • Watch for weight and muscle loss. That risk is already elevated in cancer patients and can grow under a calorie-restricted ketogenic protocol, which is why oncology-dietitian supervision matters.
  • Don't decide this alone. If you're on active treatment, talk to your oncology team before starting, and if possible do it within a monitored clinical trial setting.

Limitations

The ERGO2 trial involved only 50 patients, and the control group's lower-than-planned calorie intake muddies the comparison; its results apply specifically to recurrent malignant glioma and don't generalize directly to other cancer types. The Weill Cornell mouse study isn't human data, and how the mechanism plays out across different species and cancers remains uncertain. KEATING and similar pilot studies were designed primarily around safety and feasibility, not statistical power to detect efficacy. Across the field, there are still too few large, randomized, multi-center trials with adequate sample sizes; larger studies in the coming years could shift this picture.

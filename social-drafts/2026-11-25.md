**Önizleme satırı:** Türkçe'de hiç yazılmamış bir konu, 1971'den bir Japon dermatoloğun tanımı ve karbonhidrat eklemenin neden işe yaradığı.

Bu haftaki yazı biraz farklı bir yerden çıktı. Konuyu araştırmaya başlama sebebim basit: "keto döküntüsü" yazıp Google'da arattım ve Türkçe tarafta pratikte hiçbir şey yoktu. Oysa İngilizce kaynaklarda durumun bir adı, bir tarihi, hatta vaka raporları var.

Durumun tıbbi adı prurigo pigmentosa. İlk kez 1971'de Japon dermatolog Nagashima tarafından tanımlandığı için literatürde bazen Nagashima hastalığı olarak da geçiyor. Keto'ya başladıktan günler ya da aylar içinde boyun, göğüs veya sırtta kaşıntılı, kırmızı-mor kabarcıklar şeklinde beliriyor, zamanla kendine özgü bir ağ deseni oluşturuyor.

Asıl merak ettiğim şey bağlantısıydı: neden keto ile ilişkilendiriliyor? Cevap şu — kesin mekanizma hâlâ bilinmiyor, ama durum vücudun yağı yakıt olarak kullandığı her ketoz durumunda (ketojenik diyet, kontrolsüz diyabet, uzun oruç, bariatrik cerrahi sonrası, anoreksiya) tetiklenebiliyor. Bir vaka raporunda, sıkı bir ketojenik diyete başlayan 26 yaşında sağlıklı bir kadında döküntü üç hafta sonra çıkmış, karbonhidratı geri eklemesinin ardından bir ay içinde hiçbir ilaç kullanmadan tamamen geçmiş.

Cleveland Clinic'in önerisi de bu yönde: ketozdan kaynaklanıyorsa günde en az 50 gram karbonhidrat genellikle durumu düzeltiyor. İşe yaramazsa ya da karbonhidrat eklemek istemiyorsanız, doktorlar enfeksiyon olmasa da anti-inflamatuar etkisi için minosiklin, doksisiklin gibi antibiyotikler önerebiliyor.

Benim için en çarpıcı nokta, vakaların büyük kısmının genç kadınlarda ve Japonya kaynaklı literatürde bildirilmiş olması — ortalama başlangıç yaşı 23-27. Ama erkeklerde, çocuklarda, farklı etnik gruplarda da görülebiliyor, sadece daha nadir.

## Medium

**Title:** The Keto Side Effect With a Name Nobody Talks About
**Subtitle:** It's not keto flu, it's not an allergy, and there's a 1971 case report behind it

A few weeks into a strict keto diet, some people notice something they weren't warned about: an itchy, reddish-pink rash spreading across the neck, chest or back. It's not keto flu, and when it's googled, it often gets mistaken for an allergic reaction or eczema. It has an actual name in the dermatology literature — prurigo pigmentosa, nicknamed "keto rash."

The condition was first described in 1971 by a Japanese dermatologist named Nagashima, which is why it's sometimes called Nagashima disease in medical papers. It's rare, inflammatory, and develops in recognizable stages. Early on, small itchy red-to-purple bumps show up. They can grow into raised patches, then fill with fluid like tiny blisters, crust over, and settle into a distinctive net-like pattern. Eventually the itching and redness fade, but dark marks can linger for months if the rash isn't addressed.

What makes this interesting from a keto standpoint is the trigger. The exact mechanism is still unclear, but the condition shows up across essentially every state where the body switches to burning fat for fuel — not just ketogenic diets, but uncontrolled diabetes, extended fasting, the period following bariatric surgery, and anorexia nervosa. One working theory is that carb restriction changes the gut microbiome enough to trigger low-grade inflammation, though that's a hypothesis rather than a confirmed cause. The rash has also turned up for reasons that have nothing to do with ketosis at all — H. pylori infection, pregnancy-related hormone shifts, allergic reactions to metals, and in rare cases alongside autoimmune conditions like Sjögren's syndrome.

A published case report gives a useful real-world timeline. A healthy 26-year-old woman started a strict ketogenic diet and developed the rash just three weeks later. There was no medication involved in her recovery — she added carbohydrates back into her diet, and the rash cleared within a month. Cleveland Clinic frames the onset more broadly, saying it can appear anywhere from days to months after starting keto, and that it doesn't happen to everyone who tries the diet.

That carb-reintroduction approach seems to be the most consistent fix across sources. Cleveland Clinic specifically recommends a target of at least 50 grams of carbs a day if ketosis looks like the trigger. When that's not an option, or the rash doesn't respond within a few weeks, doctors sometimes prescribe antibiotics like minocycline or doxycycline, or a drug called dapsone — used here for their anti-inflammatory effect rather than to fight an infection.

One demographic detail stands out in the literature: most documented cases involve young women, heavily represented in Japanese case studies, with an average age of onset between 23 and 27. It does occur in men, children, and other ethnic groups, just less frequently and with less documentation. Whether that reflects biology or simply where the research has been concentrated is an open question.

For anyone dealing with it and hoping to stay on keto rather than quit outright, there are a few lower-effort options before escalating to medication: cutting carbs gradually instead of abruptly, starting a daily multivitamin alongside the diet change, and if the rash keeps recurring, shifting to a more moderate low-carb approach instead of strict keto. None of this is a substitute for seeing a dermatologist if the rash spreads, comes with fever, or simply doesn't improve — prurigo pigmentosa can look enough like eczema or an infection that a proper exam matters.

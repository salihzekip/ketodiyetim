Danışanlarımdan migren öyküsü olanlar sık soruyor: "Karbonhidratı kesersem migrenim de düzelir mi?" Dürüst cevap genelde "belki, ama" ile başlıyor. Çünkü elimizde gerçek klinik çalışmalar var, ama hepsi küçük ve hepsi farklı bir açıdan bakıyor.

En çarpıcı sonuç 2021'den. İlaca yanıt vermeyen, aynı zamanda ilaç aşırı kullanımı başağrısı da olan kronik migren hastalarında aylık migren günü medyanı 30'dan 7,5'e, atak süresi günde 24 saatten 5,5 saate inmiş. Kullanılan ağrı kesici dozu da üçte birine düşmüş. Bu küçük bir çalışma (23 kişi analiz edilmiş) ama rakamlar gerçek ve istatistiksel olarak anlamlı.

2015'ten bir başka çalışma, fazla kilolu 96 kadını ketojenik diyet ya da standart diyete ayırmış. Ketojenik grupta ilk ayın sonunda anlamlı iyileşme görülmüş, standart diyet grubunda görülmemiş; iyileşme 6 aya kadar sürmüş. 2023'te bu tür 10 çalışmayı birleştiren bir meta-analiz de genel olarak anlamlı bir etki bulmuş — ama çalışmalar arasında kullanılan protokol çok farklı: kimi çok düşük kalorili keto, kimi modifiye Atkins, kimi doğrudan keton takviyesi.

Mekanizma tarafında ilginç bir teori var: migren beyninin "hipometabolik" bir durumda olduğu, yani enerji kullanımında bir düzensizlik yaşadığı düşünülüyor. Ketonların bu durumu kısmen düzeltebileceği varsayılıyor — ama bu henüz kanıtlanmış değil, makul bir hipotez.

Benim çıkardığım sonuç şu: bu bir mucize değil, ama göz ardı edilecek bir bulgu da değil. Migren ilacı kullanıyorsanız dozu kendi başınıza değiştirmeyin, nörologunuzla birlikte değerlendirin. Ve keto'ya geçiş döneminin kendi baş ağrısı riski olduğunu unutmayın — ilk haftalardaki "keto gribi" gerçek bir migren atağıyla karışabiliyor.

## Medium

**Title:** What Happens When Migraine Patients Try Keto
**Subtitle:** A handful of small clinical trials asked a specific question — here's what they actually found

Migraine affects roughly one in six adults, and for women the number is closer to one in four. Given how many people that is, it's strange how little serious attention diet gets in most migraine conversations. Most of what circulates online either treats carb restriction as a trigger to avoid, or skips the topic entirely.

A handful of neurology clinics, mostly in Italy, decided to actually test it. The results are worth looking at carefully — not because they settle anything, but because they're real, specific, and more interesting than either "keto cures migraine" or "keto is dangerous for migraine."

**The most striking numbers come from drug-resistant patients**

A 2021 study in Neurological Sciences looked at chronic migraine patients who hadn't responded to medication and who also had medication overuse headache — a condition where the painkillers themselves start contributing to the pain. Fifty patients enrolled, 38 completed the protocol, 23 were included in the final analysis. The changes were substantial: median monthly migraine days fell from 30 to 7.5, median attack duration fell from 24 hours a day to 5.5 hours, and median monthly analgesic use fell from 30 doses to 6. All three changes were statistically significant.

That's a small sample, and it's a single-arm study without a control group, so it can't rule out other explanations entirely. But the magnitude of change is hard to dismiss.

**An earlier study adds a different angle**

In 2015, the European Journal of Neurology published a trial that randomized 96 overweight women with migraine into a ketogenic diet group or a standard diet group at a diet clinic. Starting from an average of 2.9 attacks a month, the keto group showed significant improvement by the end of the first month; the standard-diet group showed none. The improvement reportedly continued through month six, though it weakened somewhat during the transition period.

**What ties it together — and what doesn't**

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Nutrition pooled 10 studies using various approaches: a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet, a modified Atkins diet, a classic ketogenic diet, and direct beta-hydroxybutyrate (ketone) supplementation. Despite considerable heterogeneity between studies, the combined effect on migraine frequency and severity was statistically significant. What the review couldn't establish was a clear dose-response relationship between how deep someone's ketosis was and how much they benefited — studies measured ketosis too inconsistently to compare.

There's also a plausible, if unproven, mechanism behind all this. Imaging research suggests the migraine brain runs in a mildly "hypometabolic" state between attacks — a disruption in how efficiently it uses energy. The theory is that ketone bodies, as an alternative fuel source to glucose, might partially correct that. It's a reasonable hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism, and a 2025 review makes a point of saying so explicitly.

**The parts that matter before you'd actually try this**

Most participants across these studies were overweight women, which makes it hard to separate the effect of ketosis itself from the effect of accompanying weight loss. Sample sizes are small. Follow-up periods are short — one to six months. No large, multi-center, randomized controlled trial exists yet.

And practically: if you take migraine medication, this isn't a reason to adjust your dose on your own — that's a conversation for you and your neurologist, over time, not a decision to make from an article. The keto transition itself also tends to produce its own early headaches and fatigue (often called "keto flu"), which can be genuinely hard to distinguish from an actual migraine attack in the first couple of weeks.

None of this adds up to a recommendation that keto treats migraine. It adds up to something more modest and, honestly, more useful: a specific, testable approach with some real — if limited — clinical support, worth discussing with a doctor rather than dismissing or over-promising.

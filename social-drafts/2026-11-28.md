## Substack

**Konu başlığı:** Keto uykunu mahveder mi, yoksa düzeltir mi? İkisi de olabilir
**Önizleme satırı:** 324 kadınlı bir İtalyan çalışması, 37.000 kişilik bir ZOE verisi ve birbirine uymayan iki sonuç.

Bu hafta biraz kişisel bir yerden başlıyorum. Keto grubundaki danışanlarımdan ilk iki haftada en çok gelen şikayetlerden biri uyku. Ama ilginç olan, aynı gruptan başka insanların "hiç bu kadar iyi uyumamıştım" demesi. İkisini de ciddiye alıp literatüre baktım, çünkü bu kadar zıt anlatı bir yerde buluşmalı.

İlk bulduğum şey, 2023'te Journal of Translational Medicine'de yayımlanan İtalyan bir çalışma. 324 fazla kilolu/obez kadın, çok düşük kalorili ketojenik bir diyete (VLCKD) başlamadan önce ve 31 gün sonra Pittsburgh Uyku Kalitesi İndeksi ile değerlendirilmiş. Sonuç net: uyku kalitesinin tüm alt bileşenleri istatistiksel olarak anlamlı şekilde iyileşmiş. Sleep Foundation'ın aktardığına göre "iyi uyuyorum" diyenlerin oranı %42,9'dan %65,1'e çıkmış. Ama araştırmacıların kendi analizi şunu gösteriyor: bu iyileşmeyi açıklayan tek bağımsız etken yağ kütlesindeki azalma. Yani "diyet doğrudan uykuyu iyileştirdi" değil, "diyetin yol açtığı yağ kaybı uykuyu iyileştirmiş olabilir" demek daha doğru. Ayrıca çalışmanın kontrol grubu yok, tek merkezli ve sadece 31 gün sürmüş.

Aralıklı oruç tarafında tablo daha da karışık. 2021'de Nutrients'ta yayımlanan bir derleme, zaman kısıtlı beslenme ve gün aşırı oruç yapan insanlarla ilgili çalışmaları toplamış ve PSQI ile ölçülen uyku kalitesinde anlamlı bir değişim bulamamış. Üstüne, yazarlar incelediği çalışmaların hiçbirinin uyku sonuçlarını ölçmek için yeterli örneklem büyüklüğüne sahip olmadığını da ekliyor — yani "etkisi yok" demek de erken.

En büyük veri seti ise 2023'te Belgrad'daki Avrupa Beslenme Konferansı'nda sunulan bir King's College London çalışması. ZOE uygulamasını kullanan 37.545 kişi, iki hafta boyunca yeme pencerelerini 10 saate indirmiş ve kendi bildirimlerine göre uyku, ruh hali ve enerjide iyileşme bildirmiş. En çarpıcı detay, saatten çok tutarlılığın önemli olması: her gün aynı saatte yiyenler daha fazla fayda görmüş. Ama bu henüz hakemli yayımlanmamış bir konferans özeti ve örneklem kendi kendini seçmiş, büyük ölçüde kadın ve ortalama yaşı 60 civarında.

Benim çıkardığım sonuç şu: ilk haftalardaki uykusuzluk gerçek ve genelde elektrolit/su kaybıyla ilgili, geçici. Uzun vadede uykunun iyileşip iyileşmeyeceği ise büyük ölçüde kilo kaybına ve yeme saatlerinizin ne kadar tutarlı olduğuna bağlı görünüyor — en azından şu ana kadarki veriler böyle işaret ediyor.

## Medium

**Title:** Does Keto Wreck Your Sleep, or Fix It? The Honest Answer Is Both
**Subtitle:** What a 324-woman Italian trial, a 37,000-person ZOE dataset, and a dry academic review actually found

There are two keto origin stories about sleep, and both keep showing up in the same comment sections. One person swears the first two weeks on keto were the worst insomnia of their life. Another, often in the same thread, says they've never slept better. Neither is lying, and it turns out the research explains why both can be true at once.

Start with the strongest human dataset on the keto side: a 2023 trial published in the Journal of Translational Medicine by Barrea and colleagues. They put 324 women with overweight or obesity on a very low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) and measured sleep with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index before starting and again after 31 days. Every single sleep subscore improved significantly. According to Sleep Foundation's coverage of the paper, the share of women reporting good sleep quality rose from 42.9% at baseline to 65.1% a month later.

Here's the part that matters, though: the researchers ran a regression analysis to figure out what actually drove the improvement, and the answer wasn't "the diet" in some direct sense — it was fat loss. A drop in fat mass greater than 8.4% was the one variable that predicted better sleep. So the honest headline isn't "keto fixes your sleep," it's "losing a meaningful amount of body fat, by whatever means this diet achieved it, seems to come with better sleep." And there's a real limitation sitting underneath all of it: no control group, single site, open-label, 31 days, women only. A real signal, but an early one.

Now flip to intermittent fasting, where the picture gets considerably less tidy. A 2021 review in Nutrients, by McStay and colleagues, pulled together the available human trials on time-restricted eating and alternate-day fasting. The finding: sleep quality and sleep duration, measured with the same PSQI tool, showed no significant change across these trials. Effects on how long it took people to fall asleep, and how efficiently they slept, were inconsistent from one study to the next — worse in one, unchanged in others. The authors add a line that undercuts strong claims in either direction: none of the trials they reviewed had enough statistical power to detect a real difference in sleep outcomes even if one existed. Translation: the data can't currently tell you fasting helps sleep, but it also can't tell you it doesn't.

Then there's the biggest number in this entire topic, and the one that needs the most caveats. At the 2023 Federation of European Nutrition Societies conference in Belgrade, King's College London researchers presented data from 37,545 users of the ZOE nutrition app. After a baseline week of normal eating, participants narrowed their daily eating window to 10 hours (about 14 hours of fasting) for two weeks. Self-reported mood, energy, hunger, and sleep all improved — and the detail that stood out most wasn't the exact timing, but consistency: people who ate within the same window every single day reported more benefit than people who shifted it around. It's a genuinely interesting signal. It's also a conference abstract, not yet peer-reviewed, drawn from a self-selected group of app users whose most engaged subset was mostly women with an average age around 60. Big numbers don't automatically mean representative ones.

Why does the early-keto insomnia happen at all, if the longer-term picture can be positive? The leading explanation isn't really about ketones — it's plumbing. Cutting carbs sharply drops insulin, which makes the kidneys hold onto less sodium, which pulls water (and potassium and magnesium) out with it. That shift shows up as trouble falling asleep, fragmented sleep, and restlessness in the first one to two weeks for a lot of people, and it tends to fade as the body re-equilibrates — some sources say within days, others say it can take up to four weeks.

There's also a more speculative layer worth a mention, mostly because it's the most common thing you'll hear in keto forums: the idea that ketones directly boost deep, slow-wave sleep. That claim traces back to a 2021 theoretical review proposing a mechanism, not a clinical trial that tested it in people. An older study in children with epilepsy adds a genuinely curious data point from a different angle — the ketogenic diet reduced total sleep time in those kids while preserving slow-wave sleep and increasing REM, a "less but better" pattern. Whether that generalizes to a healthy adult cutting carbs for weight loss is a different question entirely, and not one these studies answer.

Smaller clinical studies in specific patient groups add texture without changing the overall picture. A 6-month ketogenic diet trial in people with relapsing multiple sclerosis (45 enrolled, 39 completed) reported improved self-reported sleep quality. A retrospective Italian pilot in people with chronic migraine found a 2:1 ketogenic diet beat a low-glycemic-index diet on sleep quality and daytime sleepiness. Both are real findings in real people — and both are small, short, and short of a control group, which is a pattern you'll notice repeats across almost everything in this space.

Put it all together and the fairest summary is this: short-term sleep disruption when starting keto is a real, reasonably well-documented phenomenon, mostly tied to electrolyte and fluid shifts, and it's usually temporary. Whether keto or intermittent fasting improves sleep over the long run is a much newer and shakier claim — plausible, partly supported, but resting on small or unreviewed studies rather than the kind of randomized, adequately powered trials that would settle it.

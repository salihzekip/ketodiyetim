## Substack

**Konu başlığı:** Diyabetin "ilerleyici hastalık" olduğu hikayesine bir itiraz
**Önizleme satırı:** 262 kişilik bir grupta HbA1c iki yıl boyunca düşük kaldı — ama çalışmanın kendi sınırlamaları da en az sayılar kadar önemli.

Bu haftaki yazı biraz farklı bir yerden geliyor. Danışanlarımdan biri geçen hafta "diyabetim zaten ilerleyecek, keto bir şey değiştirmez mi" diye sordu. Cevap vermeden önce konuyu araştırmaya oturdum ve ABD'li Virta Health'in yayımladığı iki çalışmayı bulup tekrar okudum.

Birinci çalışma 2018'de Diabetes Therapy'de yayımlanmış: 262 kişi düşük karbonhidratlı, ketoz hedefli bir bakım modeline katılmış, 87 kişi de her zamanki bakımını sürdürmüş. Bir yılın sonunda ilk grupta HbA1c ortalama %7,6'dan %6,3'e düşmüş, ortalama 13,8 kilo kaybı yaşanmış. İnsülin kullanan 78 kişiden 31'i insülini tamamen kesmiş, kalanlar da dozunu yarıya yakın indirmiş.

İkinci çalışma, aynı grubun 2 yıl sonraki halini gösteriyor — 2019'da Frontiers in Endocrinology'de yayımlanmış. Etki sürmüş: HbA1c %6,7'de kalmış, kontrol grubunda ise aslında yükselmiş (%7,5'ten %7,9'a). Katılımcıların %53,5'i "reversal" denen kriteri, yani metformin dışında ilaç kullanmadan HbA1c'nin %6,5'in altında olmasını karşılamış.

Burada durup iki şeyi net söylemek istiyorum. Birincisi, bu randomize bir çalışma değil — katılımcılar hangi gruba gireceğini kendileri seçti, bu da sonuçları etkileyebilecek bir faktör. İkincisi, çalışma Virta Health tarafından finanse edildi ve yazarların bir kısmı şirketin çalışanı veya hissedarı. Bu, verilerin yanlış olduğu anlamına gelmiyor, ama okurken akılda tutulması gereken bir nokta.

Asıl önemli uyarı da şu: ilaç dozundaki o büyük düşüş kendi başına denenecek bir şey değil. Bu, sürekli doktor takibiyle, kan şekeri yakından izlenerek yapıldı. İnsülin veya sülfonilüre kullanan biri karbonhidratı aniden düşürüp ilacı aynı dozda tutarsa, ciddi bir hipoglisemi riskiyle karşı karşıya kalabilir.

## Medium

**Title:** What Happens to Type 2 Diabetes After Two Years on Keto — According to the Numbers
**Subtitle:** A non-randomized trial funded by the company running it still has something real to say, as long as you read the fine print too

Ask someone with type 2 diabetes what their doctor told them at diagnosis, and you'll often hear some version of the same sentence: this is a progressive disease. Medication tends to go up over time, not down. A set of trials published by Virta Health, a US-based remote care company, offers a genuine challenge to that script — not a miracle cure, but a documented, two-year reversal in a meaningful share of participants.

The first study, published in Diabetes Therapy in 2018, enrolled 262 people in a continuous care intervention built around a low-carbohydrate diet targeting nutritional ketosis, alongside remote medical supervision and health coaching. A comparison group of 87 people kept their usual care. After one year, the intervention group's HbA1c fell from 7.6% to 6.3%, average weight loss was 13.8 kg, and the comparison group showed no significant change in either measure. Among people on insulin, 94% either reduced their dose or stopped it entirely — 31 of 78 completers stopped insulin altogether, and the rest cut their average daily dose from 105.2 to 53.8 units. Sulfonylurea use went from 23.7% to zero.

The second study, led by Athinarayanan and colleagues and published in Frontiers in Endocrinology in 2019, followed the same cohort out to two years. The effect held: HbA1c in the intervention group settled at 6.7%, while the comparison group's HbA1c actually climbed, from 7.5% to 7.9%. At two years, 53.5% of the intervention group met a "reversal" criterion — HbA1c under 6.5% with no medication besides metformin — and 17.6% reached partial or complete remission, meaning no diabetes medication at all. The comparison group's numbers barely moved from baseline.

None of this is as clean as it sounds in a headline, though. The authors describe their own design plainly: open-label, non-randomized, controlled. Participants chose which group to join rather than being randomly assigned, which means people more motivated to try a low-carb approach may have self-selected into better outcomes. The trial ran at a single site, and most participants were white, both of which limit how far the results generalize. By year two, roughly a quarter of each group had dropped out of the study. And the most important caveat: Virta Health funded the research, and several authors are company employees, founders, or hold stock options in it. That's a real conflict of interest, not a disqualifying one, but one worth sitting with.

There's also a lipid wrinkle worth knowing about. Over the first year, triglycerides fell 24% and HDL rose 18%, both favorable. But calculated LDL cholesterol rose about 10%, even though ApoB, often considered the more reliable particle-count marker, didn't change significantly. That's not a simple story, and it's one reason anyone trying this approach should keep an eye on their full lipid panel, not just LDL in isolation.

The most important thing to understand practically is this: the dramatic insulin and medication reductions in this trial weren't something people did on their own. They happened gradually, under continuous remote monitoring, with a doctor adjusting doses as blood sugar actually responded. Cutting carbohydrates sharply while keeping a fixed insulin or sulfonylurea dose is a genuine hypoglycemia risk. Anyone with type 2 diabetes considering a low-carb approach needs to loop in their doctor before changing anything, not after.
